The 24 Mountains: How the Feng Shui Compass Reads Your Home

If you have ever looked at a luo pan and wondered what the second ring means, this is the article. The 24 Mountains are the ring that splits the compass into 15-degree sectors, each one tied to a Heavenly Stem, an Earthly Branch, or a special marker. Today, June 21, is Xia Zhi — one of the four classical annual checkpoints for re-aligning the reading. So today is a good day to learn what the ring is, and what the ring sees.

Published June 21, 2026 · 9 min read

What the 24 Mountains are, in plain language

The 24 Mountains (二十四山) are the second ring on a standard luo pan, sitting just inside the outer 360-degree ring. Where the outer ring is just a Western compass, the 24 Mountains ring divides the full circle into 24 equal sectors of 15 degrees each. Each sector carries a name — either a Heavenly Stem, an Earthly Branch, or one of the four special markers (Xu, Jiao, Gong, Wei) that fill the gaps between the regular stem-branch cycle.

That gives you 12 branches, 8 of the 10 stems (the 10 stems are split, 5 to the yang half of the ring and 5 to the yin half), and 4 markers. The math comes out to 12 + 8 + 4 = 24. Each sector is centered on a specific compass degree: due south is the Wu (午, Horse) mountain at 180 degrees, due north is the Zi (子, Rat) mountain at 0 degrees, due east is Mao (卯, Rabbit) at 90, due west is You (酉, Rooster) at 270. The other 20 mountains fill in between.

The reason this ring matters is that almost every compass-school technique reads direction by mountain, not by raw degree. When the practitioner says "the south sector of the home," they usually mean the Wu mountain — the 15-degree slice from 172.5 to 187.5, not the geometric point at 180. When the Flying Stars map puts a star in the south, it is sitting in the Wu mountain. When the Tai Sui prescription says "avoid renovations in the south," the avoidance zone is the Wu mountain, not the exact point. The 24 Mountains ring is the resolution at which the compass school operates.

The full ring, north to south and back

Reading the ring from due north (0 degrees) clockwise gives you the standard sequence. The four cardinal branches come first at 0, 90, 180, and 270. The intercardinal branches fill the diagonals at 45, 135, 225, and 315. The eight "stem" mountains sit in the gaps between the branches, four in the eastern arc and four in the western arc. The four special markers (Xu, Jiao, Gong, Wei) sit at the very front of each cardinal direction, as the "leading edge" of the cardinal mountain.

Why 15 degrees, and not 30 or 10

The 15-degree resolution is not arbitrary. It is the smallest slice the Chinese astronomical tradition could resolve with the instruments they had, and it lines up with the 12-branch cycle (360 / 12 = 30 for the 12 branches alone, 360 / 24 = 15 once you add the intercardinal stems and the four markers). A finer ring (10 degrees, 5 degrees) would let you "see" more, but it would also make the readings unstable — the practitioner would be reading micro-sectors that swing depending on where they stand. 15 degrees is the practical resolution at which the human body can hold a compass steady and read a consistent mountain.

You can also think of the 15-degree slice as the resolution at which the home is stable. A 5-degree slice moves when you walk to the other side of the room. A 15-degree slice does not. The room sits in a mountain, and a 15-degree mountain is about the size of a small room, which is the resolution at which the prescription is actionable.

How a practitioner uses the ring on a real home

The actual reading goes something like this. The practitioner stands at the center of the home (or the center of the room, for room-level work) and holds the luo pan level. The outer 360-degree ring gives them a Western compass reading, which they use to verify that the luo pan is set correctly relative to magnetic north. The 24 Mountains ring then gives them the named mountain for each compass direction of the home.

For a north-facing home, the front door sits in the Zi mountain (north, 0 degrees). For a south-facing home, the front door sits in the Wu mountain (south, 180 degrees). For a southeast-facing home, the door sits somewhere in the Si or Chen mountain (the 15-degree slices around 142.5 degrees). The reading continues around the home: the kitchen sits in whatever mountain the kitchen falls into, the bedroom in whatever mountain the bed faces, and so on.

Once the practitioner has the mountain map of the home, the prescriptions start. The 2026 Flying Stars, the 2026 Tai Sui, the annual 5 Yellow, the household's natal chart, the San He triple-harmony positions, the year-pillar reading — all of these are read against the mountain map, not against the raw compass. The ring is the canvas; the rest of the system is the paint.

A practitioner reads the home as a mountain map, not a degree map. Once the map is in hand, the year's prescriptions (Flying Stars, Tai Sui, 5 Yellow) are layered on top of it. The 24 Mountains ring is the canvas the rest of the system paints on.

What the four markers (Xu, Jiao, Gong, Wei) are for

The four markers are the part of the ring that confuses most beginners, because they look like stems but are not part of the regular stem cycle. They are placeholders for the four "corners" of the cardinal directions — the leading edge of each cardinal mountain. Xu (戌) sits at 22.5 degrees, just before Zi (north). Jiao (角) sits at 67.5 degrees, just before Chou (NEE). Gong (宮) sits at 142.5 degrees, just before Mao (east). Wei (未) sits at 247.5 degrees, just before Wu (south). They carry the residual energy of the corner, and a few traditional techniques (especially the older form-school practitioners) read them as significant in their own right.

For most modern practitioners the markers are vestigial — they are filled in because the math needs them, but they are not read as actively as the branches and stems. If you are reading at the level of "which mountain does this wall face," the markers rarely matter. If you are doing a day-pillar analysis or an annual Flying Stars reading, they sometimes do.

Why today, Xia Zhi, is one of the four classical checkpoints

There are four solar terms in the classical Chinese year that are treated as checkpoints for re-aligning the luo pan reading: Li Chun (立春, around Feb 4) at the start of the year, Xia Zhi (夏至, around June 21) at the peak of summer, Li Qiu (立秋, around Aug 7) at the start of autumn, and Dong Zhi (冬至, around Dec 21) at the peak of winter. Each of these is the moment when the sun crosses a cardinal turning point in its annual path, and the classical reading is that the year's directional energy is most "set" at these moments.

Xia Zhi, today, is the year's longest day in the northern hemisphere. The sun reaches its highest noon altitude, and the day's length is at maximum. The classical reading is that the sun's energy is most "Yang" at this moment, and the world's qi is most expanded. For a luo pan reading, the implication is that the south sector of the home (the Wu mountain, the seat of maximum Yang) is at peak expression. It is the day to take a fresh reading of where the south sector actually falls in the home, and to confirm the reading against last February's Li Chun baseline.

Most practitioners do their annual compass reading at Li Chun, when the new year-pillar takes effect. The other three checkpoints are for re-confirmation — if the home has shifted (a new addition, a new fence, a new neighbor building that blocks the southeast view), the Xia Zhi reading is when the shift shows up. If nothing has changed, the Xia Zhi reading should match the Li Chun reading within a degree or two. If it does not, something is off — either the practitioner is standing in a different spot, or the home has actually moved (rare, but it happens with extensions).

What to do today, if you want to read your own home

If you have a luo pan, today is a good day to take a reading. Stand at the center of the home, hold the luo pan level, and let the needle settle. The 24 Mountains ring should be aligned so that the red needle points to "South" on the outer ring. The mountain that falls under the needle's south end is your Wu mountain. Walk to the south wall of the home, hold the luo pan against the wall, and confirm the mountain. It should be Wu, or one of the two mountains adjacent to Wu (Ding at 277.5–292.5, or Wei at 292.5–307.5) if the wall is slightly off-axis.

If you have a phone compass, the same exercise works at lower resolution. The phone compass reads degrees, not mountains, but you can translate: 172.5 to 187.5 is the Wu mountain, anything within that 15-degree slice is south. Most phone compasses are accurate to within 2 to 3 degrees, which is enough to identify a mountain but not enough to draw fine distinctions between adjacent mountains.

If you do not have either, the home is still legible without a compass. Stand in the south-facing room and face the south wall. The wall you are facing is in the Wu mountain, by definition. The reading does not need to be precise to the degree; the prescription (keep the south quiet in 2026) is the same whether the wall is at 178 degrees or 182 degrees.

What the 24 Mountains let you see, that the raw compass does not

The raw compass reads the world in degrees, and that is the right resolution for a sailor or a surveyor. The 24 Mountains ring reads the world in mountains, and that is the right resolution for a home. The shift from degrees to mountains is a shift from "where is the wall pointing" to "what kind of energy is the wall sitting in," and that shift is the whole reason the compass school of feng shui exists.

A wall that points exactly to 180 degrees sits in the Wu mountain, the south, the Horse, the Yang Fire sector, the seat of the 2026 Tai Sui. A wall that points to 175 degrees still sits in the Wu mountain, and the prescription is identical. A wall that points to 165 degrees sits in the Yi mountain, the Yin Wood sector, and the prescription is different. The 15-degree ring is the resolution at which the prescription changes; the 1-degree ring is the resolution at which the practitioner wobbles.

The ring is also the resolution at which the practitioner talks to the client. When a practitioner tells a client "your kitchen is in the south sector," the client knows to point at the south side of the home. When a practitioner tells a client "your kitchen is at 184 degrees," the client stares blankly. The mountain is the resolution that maps onto lived experience, and the 24 Mountains ring is the format that makes the system usable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a real luo pan, or will a phone compass do?
A phone compass is fine for room-level work. It reads degrees, not mountains, but you can translate: each mountain is a 15-degree slice, and the four cardinal mountains (Zi, Mao, Wu, You) are easy to identify by their degree ranges. A real luo pan adds resolution (you can read the stem mountains, not just the branches) and tradition (the practitioner holds the same instrument the tradition was built around), but for the basic "which side of the home faces south" question a phone compass is enough.
Why 15 degrees and not 30 or 45?
The 15-degree slice comes from the math: 360 divided by 24 mountains equals 15. The 24-mountain ring is the natural resolution of the 12-branch cycle once you add the 8 in-between stems and 4 corner markers. A coarser ring (30 degrees, the 12-branch cycle alone) loses the intercardinal branches; a finer ring (5 or 10 degrees) reads micro-sectors that wobble when the practitioner moves. 15 degrees is the practical resolution at which the instrument, the tradition, and the home all line up.
My home faces 10 degrees off true north. Does that change the reading?
Yes, slightly. A 10-degree offset means your "south" is actually at 190 degrees, which falls in the Chen mountain (Dragon, ESE) rather than the Wu mountain (Horse, true south). Most apartment buildings have small offsets like this, and the practitioner accounts for them. The reading is the same in spirit (a south-facing home has its door in the year's sensitive sector), but the exact mountain is shifted by one position. If you have a luo pan or a phone compass, take 30 seconds to check the offset before you read the home.
What is the difference between the 24 Mountains and the bagua?
The bagua is a 9-sector map of the home (the 8 trigrams plus the center) used for the form school and the bagua-map overlays. The 24 Mountains are a 24-sector ring on the compass used for the compass school. The two systems use different geometries — the bagua divides the home into 9 equal sectors, the 24 Mountains divide the compass into 24 equal slices. They are complementary, not interchangeable. Most practitioners read the home with both, layering the bagua (life areas, family members) on top of the mountain map (compass direction, energy type).
Can I read the 24 Mountains ring without learning the Chinese characters?
Yes. The mountain names have pinyin (Zi, Wu, Mao, You, etc.) and English translations (Rat, Horse, Rabbit, Rooster). The 12 branches are the 12 zodiac animals, and most English readers already know the animal names. The 8 stems are numbered 1 through 8 in some traditions (Jia = 1, Yi = 2, Bing = 3, etc.) and named after their elements in others. The four markers (Xu, Jiao, Gong, Wei) are the only ones that need a little memorization, and most practitioners skip them in basic readings.
Is there a 24 Mountains app?
There are several. Most feng shui compass apps overlay the 24 Mountains ring on a phone compass, and the better ones (search "feng shui compass" or "luo pan app") let you set magnetic declination for your location. The apps are useful for a quick reading but they do not replace a real luo pan for serious work, because the phone compass drifts and the phone itself is metal, both of which add small errors to the reading. For a one-time home reading, an app is enough. For ongoing work, a real luo pan is worth the investment.
