6 Feng Shui Mistakes Everyone Makes (Your Mirror Is Probably Wrong)

Most of what shows up under "feng shui" on a Pinterest board is a fragment of a real classical idea, with the part that made it work cut off. The mirror faces the wrong way. The plant sits in the wrong corner. The fountain makes a sound no one can hear from across the room. Each fix on its own is small, but stacked together they pull a home in a direction the homeowner did not ask for. Today's piece is a list of the six moves I see most often, the classical reason each one fails, and the cleaner prescription that does the job it was advertised to do.

Published June 27, 2026 · 11 min read

Why these mistakes keep showing up

The classical feng shui corpus is large and largely Chinese. The English-language version of the practice that has been popular since the 1990s is a compressed translation. A practitioner trained in the Form School or the Compass School will work from texts that run into the hundreds of pages, with footnotes and exceptions for every general rule. A homeowner reading an interior design blog gets the headline version: mirror faces the door, plant in the wealth corner, fountain in the southeast. The headline is correct in shape. It is missing the conditions.

The conditions matter more than the headline. A mirror facing the front door pushes qi back out of the home in a particular kind of house — a small entry, a door that opens directly into a stair. In a different house — a long hallway, a door that opens onto a wall four feet away — the same mirror does the opposite of what was intended, because the qi never gets into the home in the first place. The cure is the cure. The context decides whether it works.

The six mistakes below are not exotic. They are the ones that show up in apartment consultations, in pre-purchase walkthroughs, and in the photos that get saved to a homeowner's "feng shui ideas" board. Each one has a classical reason it fails and a classical fix that does the job. None of them require a renovation. Most of them require moving one object or removing one.

A feng shui cure is a conditional instruction, not a decoration. The condition is the part that got cut off when the practice was translated for an English-speaking audience. The decoration is what survived.

The six moves that look right and read wrong

Mistake 1

Mirror directly facing the front door

The popular version says a mirror across from the front door pushes qi back out of the home. The classical version is more specific, and the conditions matter more than the headline. A mirror across from the front door is correct in one configuration and wrong in three. It is correct in a long, narrow hallway with the door at one end, where the mirror at the other end bounces light back down the hall and stops the qi from traveling the length of the corridor. It is wrong in a small entry hall where the door opens directly into the room, because the mirror amplifies whatever the door sees — a wall, a kitchen, a stair — which is rarely what the homeowner wants amplified. It is wrong in a T-junction where the front door opens onto a hallway with rooms on either side, because the mirror multiplies the cross-traffic at the door. And it is wrong when the mirror is large enough to reflect the homeowner leaving the home, because the classical reading is that the mirror "sends the owner out."

Cleaner prescription: Mirror on a side wall, not on the wall directly facing the door, and only when the entry is long enough to need it. A small entry hall gets a piece of art or a console table instead. The mirror's job is to extend the path of qi into the home, not to bounce it back across the threshold.
Mistake 2

Plant in the wealth corner as a default

The plant-in-the-Xiu-corner rule gets repeated so often that it has stopped sounding conditional. It is conditional on two things. The cell has to actually be the wealth cell, and the plant has to be alive. The standard Western bagua is the Post-Natal (Hou Tian) bagua, and on that map the southeast is the Xiu cell. On the Pre-Natal (Xian Tian) bagua — which some practitioners and some schools use — the southeast is the Xun cell (wind, growth), and the wealth cell is in the northwest. A homeowner who places a plant by the rule and is reading the wrong map is putting the cure in the wrong corner.

The second condition is harder. A plant that is failing (yellow leaves, dry soil, root rot) is not a wealth cure. It is a sign that the corner cannot support life, which in classical reading means the corner's qi is weak or blocked, which means the cure is the wrong cure for that corner. A silk plant is not a substitute either. The classical prescription requires living qi in the cell, and a silk plant carries no qi at all.

Cleaner prescription: First, decide which bagua the home is being read on (most American homes are read on the Post-Natal map, but confirm with the practitioner). Second, choose a plant suited to the light in the chosen corner (a southeast corner with low light does not want a ficus). Third, accept that a plant is a recurring commitment, not a one-time cure. If the homeowner is not going to water it, the cure should be a small ceramic object in a warm color (gold, ochre, terracotta) rather than a living plant that is going to die.
Mistake 3

Small tabletop fountain as a water cure

Most water cures I see in client homes are the wrong scale. The classical version of a water cure is a body of water large enough to be seen and heard from across the room, with enough movement to read as flowing, not as a trickle. A tabletop fountain that sits on a shelf, with a pump barely strong enough to push water over a piece of resin, does not meet the bar. The sound is too quiet to register from more than two feet away, and the visual is too small to anchor the corner it sits in. The cure is supposed to be the loudest object in the room. The fountain is not.

There is also a placement rule that the small fountain ignores. A water cure in the wealth cell is a yang-water cure — active, flowing, expanding. A water cure in the career cell (the north, on the Post-Natal map) is a yin-water cure — still, reflective, contained. The same fountain does not do both jobs. A homeowner who places the fountain by the rule and then moves it because the room layout demands a different position has lost the cure.

Cleaner prescription: Either commit to a fountain large enough to be heard from the door of the room (a floor fountain, a wall-mounted fountain, a small pond), or skip the water cure and use a dark blue or black object in the cell instead (a ceramic bowl, a glass vase, a piece of fabric). The point is to register the water element in the corner. The point is not to own a particular object.
Mistake 4

Bagua mirror on the front door

The convex bagua mirror is one of the most recognizable feng shui objects in American homes, and one of the most often misapplied. It is a real classical instrument in the Form School, but it is meant to deflect sha from an external source — a corner of a building pointing at the door, a T-junction with fast-moving traffic, a church spire or a hospital in the line of sight. It is not meant to be hung on every front door as a general protective measure. In a quiet residential street with no external sha source, the bagua mirror has nothing to deflect, and its eight trigrams broadcast outward in all directions, which is the opposite of what most homeowners want for a home that is trying to gather qi rather than push it away.

There is also a neighbor problem. A bagua mirror hung on a door facing a sidewalk is a hostile gesture in Chinese folk practice, and a homeowner who has hung one as decoration is making a statement to the street that the practice was not meant to make. In a multi-unit building, the same mirror faces the neighbor's door down the hall, which is worse.

Cleaner prescription: Use a bagua mirror only when an external sha source has been identified by a practitioner, and only after less aggressive cures have failed (a plant, a screen, a tall piece of furniture between the door and the source). For the average home with no external sha, the cure is the front door itself: a solid door, a clear threshold, a doormat outside and a small console inside. The door is the bagua. The threshold is the cure.
Mistake 5

Crystal hung in the window to "catch" light

This is the move that gets photographed most often, and it is doing two contradictory things at once. The classical version of a crystal cure is a crystal placed in a bagua cell where the element of the cell is missing or weak, not a crystal hung in a window. A crystal in a window is refracting the sunlight, which is correct for a room that needs more light activity. It is also placed in the window, which is the room's mouth — the qi of the home enters through windows and doors. The crystal at the mouth of the room is splitting the qi as it enters, which is the opposite of the cure's job. The light gets in. The qi does not.

There is a sub-mistake inside this one. The crystal cure is associated with the earth element (crystal as earth, as mineral, as a thing drawn from the ground). A window cure in a room whose missing element is wood — a missing wood room wants more plants, more green, more vertical lines — is not a crystal cure at all. The cure is mismatched to the diagnosis.

Cleaner prescription: Crystals belong in bagua cells, not in windows. A cell that wants earth (the southwest, the center, the northeast on the Post-Natal map) gets a crystal, a ceramic object, or a piece of stone. A room that wants more light activity gets a mirror placed on a side wall where it bounces daylight into the back of the room. The two cures are different and do different jobs.
Mistake 6

Wind chimes hung in every room

Metal wind chimes are correct in one configuration and harmful in three. The classical version of a wind chime cure is one wind chime, hung in one specific place, where the qi is genuinely stagnant. The metal element is associated with the west and the northwest on the Post-Natal map (children and mentors, respectively), and the cure is correct in those cells when the corner is heavy or stuck. It is not correct as a general decoration.

A home with wind chimes in the living room, the bedroom, the hallway, and the kitchen has moved from cure to noise. The qi in the home is not stagnant. It is being pushed by four metal objects, which over-corrects and creates the opposite problem: the qi cannot settle, the rooms cannot rest, sleep is light. The chime that is supposed to cure stagnation has become its own stagnation, the kind that comes from too much movement.

Cleaner prescription: One wind chime, in one room, in the corner where the qi has actually settled. A room that is alive and active does not need a chime. A room that feels heavy or stale does. The cure is also conditional on the homeowner's tolerance for sound. A chime in a bedroom is a bad cure for a light sleeper, regardless of what the room's bagua says.
The common thread, in one paragraph Each of these six moves works in a narrow context and fails outside it. The mirror works in a long hallway and fails in a small entry. The plant works in a wealth corner with good light and fails in a wealth corner with no light. The fountain works at floor scale and fails at tabletop scale. The bagua mirror works when external sha is real and fails when it is decorative. The crystal works in a bagua cell and fails in a window. The wind chime works in one stagnant room and fails in four. The classical version of each cure is conditional. The popular version is unconditional. The conditional version is the one that does the job.

How to read your own room before applying any cure

The list above is a list of mistakes, but the deeper issue is the order of operations. A cure applied before the room has been read is a guess. The classical sequence is: first, read the room's compass direction and the bagua cell that the room's front door sits in. Second, read the room's use (what activity happens in the room, at what times of day, by how many people). Third, read the room's yin-yang ratio using six features: light, sound, material, color, occupancy, and movement. Fourth, identify which single feature is loudest or weakest. Fifth, choose a cure that adjusts that single feature.

This is the order that gets skipped. The order gets skipped because the popular version of feng shui skips straight to step five (the cure) and lets the cure do the work of steps one through four. The cure cannot. A wealth corner plant in a room with bad light and a northwest-facing window is a plant in a dark corner, not a wealth cure. The cure is decorative. The room is still misread.

For most homeowners, the read can be done in fifteen minutes with a luo pan or a compass, a floor plan, and the Post-Natal bagua map. The read produces a one-paragraph diagnosis (which cell the room's front door sits in, what element the cell carries, what the room's use is, what the room's yin-yang ratio is). The diagnosis produces the cure. The cure is the last step, not the first.

What to do when the diagnosis is unclear

Sometimes the diagnosis is unclear because the room is doing two things at once (a guest room that is also a home office, a dining room that is also a homework station, a bedroom that is also a reading room). The classical response is to choose the room's primary use, the activity that happens in the room for the most hours per week, and read the room on that use. The secondary use gets a secondary adjustment. A room that is primarily a bedroom with a secondary use as a reading nook is read as a bedroom. The reading nook gets a lamp, which is a yin-yang adjustment (not a bagua adjustment).

Sometimes the diagnosis is unclear because the room has no clear bagua cell. This happens in open-plan homes where the front door opens onto a living room that contains the kitchen, the dining room, and the hallway to the bedrooms. The classical response is to read the home as a whole first — which cell does the front door sit in, what element does that cell carry — and then read each sub-area (kitchen, dining, living) as a sub-cell of the main cell. The whole-home read is louder than the sub-cell reads. A homeowner who decorates each sub-cell without reading the whole home is decorating rooms that are out of sync with the home's overall element.

The one-room check you can do in five minutes

Pick the room you spend the most waking hours in. Stand in the doorway. Look at the room as a whole for thirty seconds without naming anything. Then answer five questions, in order:

  • What is the brightest thing in the room? (If the answer is a window, the room is light-led. If the answer is a lamp, the room is lamp-led. If the answer is a mirror, the room is mirror-led.)
  • What is the loudest sound in the room at this moment, even if the answer is "almost nothing"? (Silence is a sound. It tells you the room is quiet-led, which is a yin feature.)
  • What is the softest surface in the room? (A rug, a curtain, a sofa. Soft surfaces are yin. The room's yin-yang ratio depends on how much of the floor and wall is soft versus hard.)
  • What is the room's primary color at a glance, before you start naming individual objects? (White walls with one accent color reads as a white room. White rooms are yang-led. A dark green or navy room reads as a dark room. Dark rooms are yin-led.)
  • Where is the room's qi actually entering, in your best guess? (Front door, window, hallway, all of the above. The qi entry point is the room's mouth, and the cure's first job is to make sure the mouth is open.)

The five answers together are the room's read. The read is more useful than any bagua map when the room's use is dominant (which is most rooms). A yang-led room that wants to be a reading nook needs a lamp and a soft surface, not a wealth corner plant. The read is the cure.

A feng shui read is a paragraph, not a floor plan. Five questions, answered in order, produce the paragraph. The paragraph produces the cure. The cure is the last step. The popular version starts with the cure, which is why most popular cures do not work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are all six of these mistakes in every home?
No. Most homes have two or three of them, not all six. The most common pairing is the wealth corner plant in a low-light corner plus the small tabletop fountain in the wealth corner. Both are aimed at the same cell and both fail the same way. The next most common is the bagua mirror on the front door plus a wind chime somewhere inside. The cure set is over-stacked, and the home feels busy in a way the homeowner cannot name. Removing two or three objects usually does more than adding five.
If a cure does not work, is the cure wrong or the diagnosis wrong?
Usually the diagnosis. The cure is a faithful application of a rule. The rule works in a narrow context. The context was misread. The cleanest response is to back up to step three (the yin-yang read) and check whether the room's use matches the room's element. A cure that does not produce a felt change in the room within two weeks is usually a misdiagnosis. Move the cure or remove it, do not add a second cure on top of it.
Is it bad feng shui to have a bagua mirror if nothing is pointing at the door?
Not bad, but pointless. The mirror is doing decorative work, not curative work. A practitioner who sees a bagua mirror on a quiet residential door will usually suggest removing it and replacing it with a piece of art or a small plant by the door instead. The art is decoration. The plant is qi. The mirror is neither, unless an external sha source has been identified.
Do these rules apply to apartments and small homes?
More strictly, not less. The cures above were developed for Chinese courtyard homes, which are larger and have more room for each cure to do its work without interference. An apartment is a smaller version of the same logic, and the cures have to be scaled down to match. A small fountain instead of a floor fountain. A single small plant instead of a corner plant. One wind chime, not three. The apartment is more sensitive to over-stacking, because each cure is closer to the others and the qi has less room to settle.
What is the single most useful thing a homeowner can do this week?
Remove one cure that was applied without a diagnosis. Walk through the home with a critical eye. Any object that was placed because of a feng shui rule (a mirror here, a plant there, a fountain on the shelf) gets evaluated against the five-question read from the room it sits in. If the object does not show up in the read, it is decoration, not a cure. Decoration is fine. The mistake is calling decoration a cure and then wondering why the room does not feel right. Most homes feel better after one or two objects are removed than they did when those objects were added.
Does the BaZi chart change which of these cures applies?
Indirectly, yes. The day master's element and the chart's overall element balance shift which bagua cell the household is naturally attuned to, which in turn shifts which cure set is most relevant. A Wood-heavy household reads the home through the Wood cell — which makes Wood cures (plants, green, vertical lines) more relevant than Earth cures (crystals, ceramics, stone).
