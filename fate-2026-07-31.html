Outdoor Plants Feng Shui: How Garden Trees, Shrubs, and Potted Greens Shape the July Property Read

The outdoor water-feature piece two days ago walked the dragon through the still ponds, the moving fountains, and the receiving basins at the property's lower edge. The outdoor lighting piece the day after walked the dragon along the porch lights and the path lights and the courtyard lanterns as the sun set. Today's piece walks the third outdoor feature the foundation reads always name but rarely walk through at length: the outdoor plants, the trees and shrubs and potted greens that line the property's vertical edge, hold the soil the dragon follows, and feed the Fire month through the Wood element's productive cycle. A standalone pillar piece on the six outdoor plant typologies, the four yard placement rules, the three defects, the chart-pair leaf-read, and the late-July Wood-pruning checkpoint as the Earth month settles.

Published: 2026-07-31 · Reading time: about 16 minutes

Why the outdoor plants are the read the water-feature and lighting pieces ended with

The outdoor water-feature piece walked the dragon along the property's horizontal lower edge: the still pond at the side yard, the moving fountain at the courtyard center, the receiving basin at the back patio. Each read stopped where the water met the soil. The dragon paused at the pond's edge, settled at the fountain's lip, rested at the basin's stone. Each read stopped at the ground the homeowner has planted. The outdoor lighting piece two days later walked the dragon along the property's vertical edge at night: the porch light at the gate, the path lights along the side yard, the courtyard lantern at the center. Each read stopped where the light met the leaves. The dragon settled at the porch where the bougainvillea climbs, rested at the path where the boxwood hedges stand, paused at the courtyard where the small maple spreads. Today's piece starts where the water stopped and the lighting landed: at the outdoor plants the homeowner has chosen to fill the property's vertical edge.

What the outdoor plants share with the water feature is the dragon's natural preference for living things. The dragon follows moving water more readily than still water, the dragon follows still water more readily than a stone, the dragon follows a stone more readily than a poured concrete pad. A property lined with living plants is a property the dragon follows more readily than a property lined with hardscape alone, and a property with both water and plants is a property the dragon settles into rather than walks past. What the outdoor plants share with the outdoor lighting is the dragon's natural preference for vertical reach. A path light at ground level illuminates the dragon's foot, a wall sconce at eye level illuminates the dragon's chest, a tree branch at canopy level illuminates the dragon's crown. The dragon follows light upward when the upward reach is alive (leaves, branches, blossoms) more readily than when the upward reach is inert (a wall, a fence post, a decorative pillar).

What the outdoor plants do not share with the water feature or the lighting is the homeowner's seasonal obligation. The water feature sits where the homeowner has built it; the lighting is wired where the homeowner has installed it; the plants grow, shed, get pruned, get replaced, and read differently each season. The outdoor plants are the only one of the three outdoor features that the homeowner must tend weekly to keep the read intact. A pond the homeowner stops cleaning reads as a still pond with algae, not a still pond the dragon avoids; a path light the homeowner stops changing reads as a path light with a burned-out bulb, not a path light the dragon avoids. A tree the homeowner stops pruning reads as a tree that has overgrown its placement, and a tree that has overgrown its placement is a tree the dragon reads as a defect the homeowner has stopped caring for. The outdoor plants are the read that asks the homeowner for the most attention and the most seasonal adjustment.

The outdoor plants read, in one sentenceRead the outdoor plant typology in each of the six named placements (canopy tree, ornamental tree, shrub, hedge, potted plant, climbing vine — set by the plant's mature height and root spread, not by the homeowner's current pruning). Read the leaf shape against the chart's missing element (pointed leaves feed Wood, round leaves feed Earth, needle leaves feed Metal, broad leaves feed Water, heart-shaped leaves feed Fire). Read the placement cycle against the property's three outdoor edges (a tall canopy at the back edge, a mid-height ornamental at the side edge, a low hedge at the front edge). Fix the three defects in order: a plant that overgrows its placement, a plant that has died and been left standing, a plant the homeowner planted in a defect corner without correcting the placement. The homeowner who has done the four-yard checklist has finished the outdoor plants read the water-feature and lighting pieces handed off.

The six outdoor plant typologies

Every outdoor plant falls into six typologies, set by the plant's mature height and root spread rather than by the homeowner's current pruning, the plant's nursery tag, or the homeowner's personal taste. The mature height is the height the plant reaches after seven to ten years in the soil, and the mature spread is the canopy or branch spread the plant reaches at the same maturity. The homeowner cannot change the typology without removing the plant, and the read is to plant the typology the placement has called for rather than the typology the homeowner prefers.

Canopy treeBack edge · 20+ ftA tall tree with a wide spreading crown, usually a maple, an oak, a camphor, a scholar tree (Sophora), or a ginkgo. Reads as the most dragon-defining typology because the canopy tree establishes the property's vertical anchor and casts the dragon's natural shade across the back half of the yard. The lean-into read is to keep the canopy tree at least fifteen feet from the back wall of the house (a canopy tree ten feet from the wall shades the back rooms, drops leaves onto the roof, and the dragon reads the back wall as damp rather than settled) and to keep the canopy tree's lowest branch above the homeowner's head height (a low-hanging branch over a path reads as a barrier the dragon ducks under rather than a passage the dragon walks through).
Ornamental treeSide edge · 8 to 20 ftA small flowering or fruiting tree, usually a cherry, a magnolia, a plum, a crabapple, a pomegranate, or a persimmon. Reads as the most seasonally-active typology because the ornamental tree blooms for two to four weeks each spring and bears fruit (if the homeowner has chosen a fruiting cultivar) for two months each autumn. The lean-into read is to keep the ornamental tree on the property's east or south edge (the Fire-leaning quadrant, where the ornamental's seasonal cycle aligns with the property's most-read direction) and to keep the ornamental tree's drip line clear of the homeowner's hardscape (a cherry whose drip line falls onto a patio reads as a stain the homeowner has accepted, and the dragon reads the patio as stained rather than settled).
ShrubSide edge · 3 to 8 ftA mid-height woody plant with multiple stems from the ground, usually a camellia, an azalea, a hydrangea, a gardenia, a holly, or a boxwood. Reads as the most architectural typology because the shrub frames the property's mid-level edge and draws the dragon's eye across the horizontal plane. The lean-into read is to keep shrubs in clusters of odd numbers (three camellias, five azaleas, seven boxwoods) rather than in even-numbered rows (a row of four camellias reads as a planted fence, a cluster of three reads as a settled group) and to keep the shrubs' mature spread at least four feet from the homeowner's walkway (a shrub two feet from the walkway crowds the dragon's path and reads as a barrier).
HedgeFront edge · 2 to 4 ftA row of closely-spaced low shrubs, usually a boxwood hedge, a privet hedge, a lavender hedge, or a rosemary hedge. Reads as the most boundary-defining typology because the hedge marks the property's front edge and signals the dragon's transition from the public street to the private yard. The lean-into read is to keep the hedge at a height the homeowner can see over (a hedge at chest height is a wall the dragon cannot see past, and the dragon's read at the gate is blocked) and to keep the hedge trimmed at the same height across its entire length (a hedge the homeowner has let grow tall in one corner and trimmed short in the other reads as an abandoned boundary).
Potted plantPorch · patio · balconyA plant grown in a container rather than in the ground, usually a citrus tree in a wooden tub, a small bamboo in a ceramic pot, a flowering annual in a clay bowl, or a succulent in a stone trough. Reads as the most mobile typology because the potted plant can be moved by the homeowner across the seasons (a potted citrus the homeowner carries into the garage for winter, a potted annual the homeowner rotates from the front porch to the back patio each summer). The lean-into read is to keep the pot's material matched to the plant's element (a wooden tub for a woody plant, a ceramic pot for an Earth-feeding plant, a metal trough for a Metal-feeding plant, a stone trough for a Water-feeding plant) and to keep the pot's size matched to the plant's mature size (a small bonsai in a large pot reads as a plant the homeowner has under-potted, and a large citrus in a small pot reads as a plant the homeowner has over-potted and stressed).
Climbing vineWall · trellis · arborA plant trained to grow upward along a structure, usually a wisteria on an arbor, a bougainvillea on a wall, a climbing rose on a trellis, a jasmine on a fence, or a Virginia creeper on a stone face. Reads as the most vertical-reach typology because the climbing vine covers the wall's surface and draws the dragon's eye upward along the vertical plane. The lean-into read is to keep the vine on a structure the homeowner has built for it (a vine trained directly onto a wood wall reads as a wall the homeowner has let the vine damage, and the dragon reads the wall as compromised) and to keep the vine's growth contained to the structure's footprint (a vine that has spread onto the roof or onto an adjacent wall reads as a vine the homeowner has lost control of).

Most properties have more than one typology in the yard. A back yard might have a canopy tree at the back edge, an ornamental tree at the side edge, a row of shrubs along the patio. A front yard might have a hedge at the front edge, a pair of ornamental trees flanking the gate, a potted plant on the porch. A side yard might have a hedge at the property line, a climbing vine on the wall, a row of potted plants along the path. Each typology carries its own read, and the homeowner reads each plant separately. The outdoor plants reads together form the property's full living-edge read the homeowner needs once the water feature and lighting reads are done.

The four yard placement rules

Once the outdoor plant typology is named, the read looks at the placement the homeowner has already chosen on the property. The four yard rules are the four outdoor edges the foundation reads have always used, and the rule is the same in every tradition: the dragon reads plants differently depending on which edge the plants sit on. The homeowner's job is to know which edge each plant occupies and what that edge means for the read.

Rule one: the back-edge canopy reads as the dragon's settling anchor. The first rule is to plant one canopy tree at the back edge of the property, on the side of the yard that faces the homeowner's most-used back room. The back edge is the Kun (Earth) quadrant's outdoor extension (the receiving position), and the canopy tree receives the dragon as the dragon moves from the property's center toward the back wall. A maple, a camphor, a scholar tree, or a ginkgo are all valid choices. The lean-into read is to keep the canopy tree's species matched to the climate zone (a camphor in zone 6 dies in the first hard winter, a sugar maple in zone 9 wilts through August), and to keep the canopy tree planted at the back edge rather than at the center of the yard (a canopy at the center shades the homeowner's patio, drops leaves into the homeowner's seating area, and the dragon reads the patio as overgrown rather than settled).

Rule two: the side-edge ornamentals read as the dragon's seasonal pace. The second rule is to plant at least one ornamental tree on each side edge of the property, oriented to the side that receives the most morning sun. The side edges are the Gen (Mountain) and Xun (Lake) quadrants' outdoor extensions (the transition positions), and the ornamental trees carry the dragon's seasonal pace as the year moves through bloom, fruit, leaf-fall, and dormancy. A cherry, a magnolia, a plum, a crabapple are all valid choices. The lean-into read is to keep the ornamental tree's bloom timing staggered between the two side edges (a cherry that blooms in March on the east edge paired with a crabapple that blooms in April on the west edge reads as a property with a six-week bloom window, while two cherries that bloom the same week read as a property with a one-week bloom window the dragon waits for and misses).

Rule three: the front-edge hedge reads as the dragon's boundary signal. The third rule is to keep the front-edge hedge at a height the homeowner can see over (waist height or just above) and to keep the hedge's species consistent across the front edge's full width. The front edge is the Kan (Water) quadrant's outdoor extension (the receiving-and-releasing position), and the hedge marks the dragon's transition from the public street to the private yard. A boxwood hedge, a privet hedge, a lavender hedge are all valid choices. The lean-into read is to keep the hedge's species low-allergen (a hedge the homeowner is allergic to reads as a hedge the homeowner tends from inside the house rather than walks alongside) and to keep the hedge trimmed at the same height across the entire front edge (a hedge with a gap reads as a fence the homeowner has not finished, and the dragon's read at the gate is incomplete).

Rule four: the patio and porch potted plants read as the dragon's hand-off zone. The fourth rule is to place the potted plants on the patio and the porch in matched pairs, with the pot's material matched to the plant's element and the pot's size matched to the plant's mature size. The patio and the porch are the Li (Fire) and Kan (Water) quadrants' transitional extensions (the dragon's settling-and-receiving positions), and the potted plants receive the dragon as the dragon crosses from the yard into the home. A pair of citrus in wooden tubs, a pair of small bamboo in ceramic pots, a pair of flowering annuals in clay bowls are all valid choices. The lean-into read is to keep the potted plants on the same watering schedule as the rest of the yard's plants (a potted plant the homeowner forgets to water during a dry week reads as a plant the homeowner has neglected, and the dragon's read at the patio is dry) and to keep the potted plants away from the homeowner's door swing (a potted plant directly in the door's swing path reads as a barrier the dragon must walk around).

The three defects

The three-defect walk is what the homeowner does once the typology is named, the four placement rules are checked, and the property's outdoor plants layout is read. The walk takes about thirty minutes on a quiet afternoon and covers the three defects the outdoor plants read always catches. The defects are listed in the order they appear in the homeowner's check.

Defect one: a plant that has overgrown its placement. The first defect is the most common and the most visible from the street. A canopy tree that has spread over the roof, a hedge that has grown above the homeowner's sight line, a shrub that has crowded the walkway, a climbing vine that has climbed onto the adjacent wall. The dragon sees the overgrowth and reads the property as one the homeowner has let the plants outgrow rather than one the homeowner tends. The fix is the annual prune (a once-a-year hard cut, taken in late winter for most deciduous species and after bloom for spring-flowering species) and the seasonal touch-up (a once-a-month trim through the growing season). The homeowner who prunes once and walks away reads the same property the dragon passed last year; the homeowner who prunes once and touches up monthly reads a different property each visit.

Defect two: a plant that has died and been left standing. The second defect is the most unfortunate and the most easily fixed. A shrub that died in last summer's drought and was left standing through the winter, a hedge with three dead plants in a row of seven, a potted plant the homeowner forgot to water during the August holiday and has not replaced. The dragon sees the dead plant and reads the property as one the homeowner has stopped tending. The fix is the same-day removal (a homeowner who pulls the dead plant the same day it dies reads a property the homeowner cares for; a homeowner who leaves the dead plant through the next season reads a property the homeowner has abandoned) and the same-season replacement (the homeowner who replaces the dead shrub with a new shrub of the same species and the same mature size reads a continuous edge; the homeowner who replaces a dead boxwood with an azalea reads a gap the dragon notices).

Defect three: a plant placed in a defect corner without correcting the placement. The third defect is the most subtle. A potted plant the homeowner placed in the corner where the downspout empties (the corner the homeowner has never corrected, so the downspout still empties there), a shrub planted in the corner where the air-conditioner exhausts (the corner that blasts the shrub with hot air all summer), a hedge planted against the wall where the dryer vents (the wall that dries the hedge's leaves). The dragon sees the plant in the defect corner and reads the corner as still defective; the plant has not corrected the corner, the plant has absorbed the corner's defect. The fix is either to correct the corner first (reroute the downspout, redirect the air-conditioner, vent the dryer to a different wall) and then plant the corner, or to plant a defect-tolerant species in the corner (a stonecrop in the dry corner, a holly in the cold corner, a bamboo in the wet corner). The plant that lives in a defect corner is not correcting the corner; the plant is surviving in the corner.

The chart-pair leaf-read

The six outdoor plant typologies carry the Five Elements across the property's vertical edge. A canopy tree with pointed leaves (maple, oak, camphor) reads as Wood (the productive element). An ornamental tree with heart-shaped leaves (cherry, magnolia, redbud) reads as Fire (the gathering element). A shrub with round leaves (boxwood, camellia, gardenia) reads as Earth (the stabilizing element). A hedge with needle leaves (rosemary, lavender, juniper) reads as Metal (the reflective element). A climbing vine with broad leaves (wisteria, Boston ivy, Virginia creeper) reads as Water (the awakening element). A potted plant with heart-shaped flowers (hibiscus, bougainvillea, rose) reads as Fire doubled (the gathering element reinforced). The chart's Day Master tells the homeowner which element their chart needs more of, and the outdoor plant typology the homeowner places in each placement can be chosen to feed the chart's missing element.

A chart with a Wood Day Master (Jia or Yi) benefits from Water-feeding placements (broad-leaved climbing vines on the back wall, broad-leaved potted plants on the patio) because Water feeds Wood through the Sheng cycle, and the broad leaf signals the Wood chart's missing Water. A chart with a Fire Day Master (Bing or Ding) benefits from Wood-feeding placements (pointed-leaved canopy trees at the back edge, pointed-leaved shrubs at the side edge) because Wood feeds Fire, and the pointed leaf signals the Fire chart's missing Wood. A chart with an Earth Day Master (Wu or Ji) benefits from Fire-feeding placements (heart-shaped flowering ornamentals on the side edges, heart-shaped flowering potted plants on the patio) because Fire feeds Earth, and the heart-shaped flower signals the Earth chart's missing Fire. A chart with a Metal Day Master (Geng or Xin) benefits from Earth-feeding placements (round-leaved shrubs at the side edges, round-leaved hedges at the front edge) because Earth feeds Metal, and the round leaf signals the Metal chart's missing Earth. A chart with a Water Day Master (Ren or Gui) benefits from Metal-feeding placements (needle-leaved hedges at the front edge, needle-leaved potted plants on the patio) because Metal feeds Water, and the needle leaf signals the Water chart's missing Metal.

The seasonal shift cross-check for late July

The outdoor plants read is different in summer than in autumn, and the seasonal shift cross-check is the read the homeowner does at the solar-term checkpoint. The current checkpoint is late July, three weeks after Li Qiu (July 7) and the Fire-to-Earth handoff. The Earth month is now established, and the read is to use the late-July window for the annual Wood pruning on the canopy tree and the ornamental trees. A canopy tree pruned in late July is a canopy tree that has shed its summer growth and will set its autumn growth in the cool weeks ahead. A canopy tree pruned in late August is a canopy tree that has been pruned too late, and the autumn growth will be weak. The lean-into read is to mark the late-July week on the calendar as the Wood pruning window and to take one full afternoon for the canopy tree, the two side-edge ornamentals, and any shrubs that have overreached their clusters.

The next checkpoint is around August 7-8, Li Qiu + 30 days, when the Earth month settles fully and the Metal preview begins. At that checkpoint the read shifts: outdoor plants that have been feeding the Wood element (the summer outdoor reads) shift to plants that begin feeding the Metal element (the autumn outdoor reads). A pointed-leaved canopy tree reads the same. A heart-shaped ornamental tree begins to drop its blossoms, and the read shifts from Fire-feeding to Earth-feeding (the dropped blossoms feed the soil). A round-leaved shrub begins to set its autumn buds, and the read shifts from Earth-feeding to Metal-feeding (the autumn buds signal Metal's reflective quality). The cross-check in early August is to plan the autumn bulb planting (daffodils, crocus, snowdrops) for the front-edge hedge's bare spots, and to plan the autumn shrub pruning for the side-edge shrubs that bloomed in spring.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to have all six outdoor plant typologies for the read to apply?
No. The read applies as a check of the four yard rules even when the property has only one or two typologies. A small yard with one canopy tree at the back edge, one ornamental tree at the side edge, and one hedge at the front edge has begun the outdoor plants read. The homeowner who adds a potted plant on the patio, a climbing vine on the back wall, a shrub cluster at the gate has layered the read. The outdoor plants are an addition to the read, not a requirement, just as the water feature and the outdoor lighting were additions to the property's outdoor reads.
What if I rent and cannot plant in the ground?
Use potted plants in containers the homeowner can move, and ask the landlord for written permission before planting anything in the ground. A row of potted citrus in wooden tubs along the patio reads as a planted edge, and a pair of potted bamboo flanking the door reads as a dragon-receiving pair. The portable plants read the same as the in-ground plants for the dragon's property-side settling pace, with one caveat: portable plants can be moved by the homeowner, and the homeowner who moves them every season has read a different yard each season. The lean-into read is to settle the portable plants in one position and leave them there for at least two seasons so the dragon's pace is consistent.
What about artificial plants?
Treated as a defect. The outdoor plants read depends on the living quality of the plant (the dragon's preference for living things over inert things), and an artificial plant is an inert thing the homeowner has placed where the dragon expects a living thing. The lean-into read is to use real plants throughout, and to use artificial plants only indoors where the dragon's read is to the homeowner rather than to the property. The exception is the dried-flower arrangement, which reads as a living thing the homeowner has cut and dried rather than an artificial thing the homeowner has bought, and which the dragon reads as a settled element rather than a defect.
How does the outdoor plants read interact with the water-feature and lighting reads from earlier this week?
The outdoor water feature draws the dragon to the property's lower edge, and the outdoor plants hold the soil the dragon walks across to reach the water. The outdoor lighting walks the dragon along the vertical edge at night, and the outdoor plants cast the shadows the lighting reads against. The three reads together form the property's full outdoor read: the water the dragon follows, the plants the dragon walks through, the lighting the dragon follows after dark. The homeowner's evening walk is the seamless handoff from one read to the next, and the placement match across the three reads (the water feature at the back edge paired with the canopy tree at the back edge, the path light along the side edge paired with the side-edge shrubs) is the read the dragon follows without pause.
Which leaf shape is right for the front-edge hedge?
Round leaves for an Earth-feeding hedge (boxwood, camellia), needle leaves for a Metal-feeding hedge (rosemary, lavender, juniper), and pointed leaves for a Wood-feeding hedge (small-leaved privet, holly). The hedge's leaf shape carries the property's most public-facing read, because the hedge is the first thing the dragon sees from the street. A round-leaved boxwood hedge reads as Earth's stabilizing quality (the property the dragon settles into). A needle-leaved rosemary hedge reads as Metal's reflective quality (the property the dragon walks past while sensing the homeowner's care). A pointed-leaved privet hedge reads as Wood's productive quality (the property the dragon follows toward the gate). The lean-into read is to match the hedge's leaf shape to the chart's missing element.
Should I prune in late July even if my canopy tree looks healthy?
Yes. The late-July prune is the annual reset, not a response to a defect. A canopy tree the homeowner prunes every year in late July reads as a tree the homeowner tends. A canopy tree the homeowner prunes only when it has overgrown its placement reads as a tree the homeowner corrects when forced. The lean-into read is to take one full afternoon in late July for the canopy tree, the two side-edge ornamentals, and any shrubs that have overreached their clusters, and to repeat the same walk in late February for the winter pruning (the late-July prune is for the summer growth, the late-February prune is for the winter structure).
Does the outdoor plants read change for an apartment?
Yes, in two specific ways. An apartment has no ground-level planting (the apartment's outdoor space is a balcony or a shared rooftop), so the rule-one canopy tree placement shifts to a single large potted tree on the balcony (a small citrus in a wooden tub, a small olive in a clay pot) rather than an in-ground canopy. An apartment shares walls with neighbors, so the rule-six climbing vine placement shifts to a self-standing trellis the homeowner has placed on the balcony (a vine trained directly onto the apartment wall reads as a wall the homeowner has let the vine damage, and the landlord reads the wall as compromised). The other two-thirds of the outdoor plants reads carry across property types unchanged. The three-defect walk catches the apartment's specific defects the same way it catches the house's defects.
