Front Garden Feng Shui for July: What to Do in the Fire-to-Earth Transition

The Fire Month ended yesterday. Earth Month starts on July 7. The seven days in between are when a homeowner sets the front garden up for the rest of the summer, and most of the moves are not what the garden center sells. Here is a practitioner's walk-through of the pathway, the gate, the porch light, and the one plant that does more work than any fountain.

Published July 1, 2026 · 11 min read

Why the front garden gets the July 1 article

Yesterday's close-out piece ended the indoor room walk that the Fire Month articles have been doing all month. The room walk is the inside of the house. The front garden is the room that guests, couriers, and the homeowner themselves pass through twice a day, and it is also the room that the seasonal qi enters before any other room in the home. A Fire Month that left the indoor rooms balanced can still be undermined by a front garden that is fighting the cycles. The reverse is also true: a balanced front garden carries the cycles into the house for the rest of the summer, even if the inside is still mid-correction.

The classical feng shui term for the front garden is the Ming Tang, sometimes translated as the bright hall or the bright courtyard. The Ming Tang is the open space between the gate and the front door. In a traditional courtyard house it is a paved square. In a Western suburban home it is the front path and the lawn. In an apartment it is the lobby or the walkway from the car. The geometry is the same: a defined open area between the boundary of the property and the entrance to the dwelling. The seasonal qi crosses the boundary at the gate, gathers in the Ming Tang, and enters the home at the front door. Whatever happens in that space shapes the qi that arrives inside.

The July 1 to July 7 window is the moment to set the Ming Tang up for Earth Month. The Fire element is winding down and the Earth element is gathering. A Ming Tang that is too Fire-heavy in early July reads as a season-lagging move. A Ming Tang that is already showing Earth signals reads as a season-leading move. The homeowner who walks the front garden in this seven-day window is the homeowner whose indoor Fire-to-Earth correction is supported by the outdoor space, not undermined by it.

The Ming Tang is the room the homeowner forgets about. The seasonal qi does not. It crosses the gate, gathers in the bright hall, and enters the home at the front door, in that order.

What to do this week, in the order the cycles suggest

These are the moves I usually suggest for the front garden in the first week of July. The order matters more than the specific objects. The first move is the one that affects every other move downstream. The last two are the polish, and skipping them is fine for a homeowner who is short on time.

  1. Walk the path from the gate to the front door at the time of day you usually use it. Not at noon, not at sunset. Walk it at the time you really do arrive home. The path at 6pm in early July is not the same path as the path at noon, and the feng shui read of the path is the read at the time you see it. Most homeowners walk the path at noon on a Saturday and wonder why it feels different at 9pm on a Wednesday. It is different because the light, the temperature, the plant shadows, and the wind are different.
  2. Clear the path of anything that blocks a person walking at a normal pace. A hose coiled by the gate, a pair of boots on the step, a recycling bin that has been at the side of the path for a week. The Ming Tang is the gathering space for seasonal qi, and a blocked Ming Tang is a Ming Tang that cannot gather. This is a maintenance move, not a cycle move, but the cycles do not work if the path itself is fighting them.
  3. Stand at the front door and look back at the gate. The view from the door to the gate is the view the seasonal qi has when it enters the home. If the gate is hidden behind a hedge, the qi arrives compressed. If the gate is fully visible from the door, the qi arrives open. The classical preference is a Ming Tang that is partly visible and partly screened — a low hedge, a wooden gate with gaps, a row of potted plants that frames the view without blocking it. The exact percentage is a judgement call, but the homeowner who can see the gate clearly from the door is leaving money on the table for the Earth Month.
  4. Place one Earth element in the Ming Tang before July 7. A terracotta pot, a stone bench, a clay bird bath, a ceramic planter with a low-growing herb in it. One Earth object, in a visible spot, on the path or just off it. The Earth element is the seasonal element for the next six weeks. A Ming Tang that already has an Earth anchor on July 1 is a Ming Tang that is ready for the seasonal shift on July 7. A Ming Tang that has to add the Earth element on July 8 is a week behind.
  5. Check the porch light. A working porch light that is on a motion sensor is the most under-rated feng shui object in the front garden. The porch light is the Fire element at the threshold. In Earth Month, the threshold needs a small, controlled Fire to keep the qi moving into the home. A porch light that has been burnt out for a month is a Fire absence at exactly the point where Fire is most useful. Replacing the bulb takes five minutes and is the highest-impact move on this list.

None of these are expensive. The most expensive single item is a terracotta pot, and the homeowner who has a garden already has something that will do. The total cost for the list is under fifty dollars in most markets, and most of the moves are free. The point is not the objects. The point is the order: walk the path, clear the path, see the gate, place the Earth, light the porch. Five moves, in that order, before July 7.

What the front garden is asking for in early July

This is the part most homeowner-level articles skip. The front garden has its own element balance, separate from the indoor rooms, and the balance shifts in early July. The Fire Month asked the Ming Tang for Water — a small dark feature, a navy doormat, a dark blue ceramic near the gate. The Earth Month asks the Ming Tang for Wood. Wood is the element that feeds the Earth in the Sheng cycle (Wood feeds Fire, Fire creates Earth, so Wood is the upstream element), and Wood is also the element that parts the Earth in the Ke cycle (Wood breaks up compacted Earth). A front garden that is already showing healthy plant growth in early July is doing two cycle jobs at once, which is the rare case where one element serves both directions.

The practical version: a homeowner who already has a shrub border, a hedge, a climber on the gate, or a row of potted plants is in good shape. The plants carry the Sheng direction and the Ke direction at the same time. A homeowner whose front garden is mostly hardscape — paved path, gravel border, stone planters with nothing in them — is in the harder case. The hardscape reads as Earth, and an Earth-heavy Ming Tang in Earth Month can become stagnant. The cure is one significant plant, not five small ones. A single Japanese maple in a large pot, a small lemon tree, a clumping bamboo in a planter. One plant, big enough to be the visual anchor of the front garden, alive and not silk. The size matters because the cycles read visual mass, and a small silk plant in a large pot reads as Earth pretending to be Wood, which the cycles do not accept.

The water-feature question, July edition

The Water-feature question comes up in every front garden consultation, and the answer in July is different from the answer in February. A small fountain or bird bath in the front garden is a Water element. Water is what extinguishes Fire, and the Fire Month wanted Water. The Earth Month does not want Water the same way. Water in Earth Month is a Ke-level move against the Earth: Water parts Earth, washes soil, softens the season. A homeowner who keeps the fountain running through July is making a Ke statement about the seasonal element, which is the opposite of what the Ming Tang is asking for in the next six weeks. The recommendation is to turn the fountain off in early July and leave it off until the autumn check-in. The bird bath can stay full — birds need water in summer — but a fountain that sprays water into the air is a different feng shui object than a still basin, and the homeowner who treats them as the same will read the cycles wrong for the rest of the summer.

The day-master-by-element reading for the front garden

If the homeowner knows their Day Master, the front garden reads slightly differently for each of the five elements. This is the part I find most people skip, because they assume the cycles are the same for every chart. The cycles are the same. What changes is which cycle the chart is asking for, and that depends on the Day Master.

A Wood-heavy chart — Jia or Yi Day Master, or a chart with strong Wood in the seasonal pillar — reads the Ming Tang as a Ke target. Wood parts Earth, and the Wood-heavy chart is already doing the parting job. The front garden in early July should soften the Wood: add a small metal wind chime (Metal cuts Wood), avoid the big Japanese maple, keep the fountain off. The Wood-heavy chart wants the Ming Tang to be a quiet rest, not a stimulation.

A Fire-heavy chart — Bing or Ding Day Master, or a chart with strong Fire in the summer pillars — reads the Ming Tang as a Sheng target. Fire creates Earth, so a Fire-heavy chart is already producing the seasonal element. The front garden should accept that production. Add the Earth anchor early, leave the fountain off, and let the Fire-heavy chart's natural output settle into the Ming Tang's Earth. The homeowner with a Fire-heavy chart is the one who benefits most from the terracotta pot on July 1.

An Earth-heavy chart — Wu or Ji Day Master, or a chart with strong Earth across the seasonal pillars — reads the Ming Tang as a Ke target in the opposite direction. The Earth-heavy chart is already producing Earth, and the Ming Tang does not need more. The cure is Wood: the significant plant, the climber on the gate, the hedge. An Earth-heavy chart whose front garden is mostly hardscape is asking for a Wood anchor, and the Earth Month is the moment to add it.

A Metal-heavy chart — Geng or Xin Day Master, or a chart with strong Metal in the autumn pillars — reads the Ming Tang as a quiet space. Metal collects, and the Ming Tang should be a still, defined area. The cure is the stone bench, the clay bird bath, the metal gate hardware. The Metal-heavy chart benefits from a front garden that is well-bounded and visually quiet, not stimulated.

A Water-heavy chart — Ren or Gui Day Master, or a chart with strong Water in the winter pillars — reads the Ming Tang as a Sheng target. Water feeds Wood, and Wood feeds the Earth. A Water-heavy chart whose front garden is sparse is asking for a Wood anchor in the Ming Tang to complete the Sheng chain. The plant is the load-bearing move for a Water-heavy chart in July, more so than for any other element.

The cycles are the same for every chart. What changes is which cycle the chart is asking for. The front garden reads the homeowner's Day Master before it reads the calendar.

What to undo at Li Qiu

The Fire-to-Earth transition ends on July 7. The seasonal polarity shifts that morning, and the front garden that was right for the transition window is not the front garden that is right for the next six weeks. The undo is part of the practice, and the homeowner who skips the undo is the homeowner whose July garden feels off by August.

The first thing to undo is the Water-feature decision. If the homeowner turned the fountain off in early July, leave it off. If the homeowner kept the fountain running through the transition, the July 7 review is the moment to turn it off. A Water feature running in the middle of Earth Month reads as a Ke move against the season, and the cycles do not forgive it quietly.

The second thing to undo is the late-Fire decorative objects. A red wreath on the front door, a string of warm fairy lights on the porch rail, a seasonal doormat in a warm color. The Fire decorations helped during the Fire Month. In Earth Month they are decorative, not curative, and a Ming Tang full of decorative Fire is a Ming Tang that is fighting the season. Move the warm-color doormat inside, swap the red wreath for a yellow or terracotta one, and let the porch light be the only working Fire at the threshold.

The third thing to undo is the assumption that the indoor close-out finished the work. Yesterday's piece closed out the indoor rooms. The indoor close-out does not touch the front garden. A homeowner who walked the inside of the house on June 30 and skipped the outside is the homeowner who will wonder why the indoor correction does not seem to land. The seasonal qi enters through the gate, gathers in the Ming Tang, and arrives at the front door. A Ming Tang that is still in late-Fire mode is contaminating the indoor correction no matter how thorough it was.

Frequently Asked Questions

I rent and cannot change the front garden. What do I do?
The movable moves are still yours: a doormat, a potted plant, a porch light bulb, a small ornament on the porch step. The Ming Tang in a rental is the walkway from the building's entrance to the street, and the homeowner can still place one Earth element in a visible spot. The bigger structural moves — the gate, the hedge, the path surface — are out of reach, and the cycles accept that. A rented Ming Tang with the doormat, the pot, and the porch light in the right configuration is still a Ming Tang in good order.
I have no garden. I live in an apartment with a balcony. Is the Ming Tang still the balcony?
The Ming Tang is the open space between the boundary of the property and the entrance to the dwelling. In an apartment, the boundary is the apartment's front door, and the Ming Tang is the hallway or the corridor outside. The balcony is a separate feng shui object — it is a Yang space, often read as a Fire or Wood element depending on what is on it. The hallway outside the apartment door is where the seasonal qi gathers before the homeowner walks in. A small doormat, a single plant in a pot, and a working light in the hallway (if the building allows) is the apartment version of the front-garden walk-through.
My front garden faces north. Does the facing direction change the reading?
Yes, but the seasonal moves are mostly the same. A north-facing front garden is in the Water phase of the compass. In early July, the Water-facing Ming Tang is the rarest case where a small water feature is still appropriate — a still bird bath is fine, a fountain is still Ke against the season. A south-facing front garden is in the Fire phase, and the Fire-to-Earth transition is more pronounced. The moves above are written for a generic east- or south-facing front garden. A north-facing homeowner should add the small Water object and reduce the Fire decorative objects even further.
What about the back garden? Does it get the same walk-through?
The back garden is the Fu Wei, the steady back area, and it has a different reading. The front garden is the seasonal qi entry. The back garden is the homeowner's relationship to the work they have already done. The July 1 walk-through is for the front garden. The back garden gets its own piece later in the month, when the Earth Month has settled and the homeowner can read the back garden in the new seasonal context. Trying to do both in the same week is the classic July mistake.
I have a bagua map overlay. Does the front garden fall into a specific bagua sector?
It depends on which bagua school the homeowner uses. The Classical bagua (the Pre-Heaven or Fu Xi arrangement) puts the front garden in the Kan sector, the Water sector, regardless of facing direction. The Western bagua (the Post-Heaven or King Wen arrangement) puts the front garden in the sector that matches the facing direction. Most homeowners use the Western bagua, and the sector depends on the compass reading. The five moves above are written for the homeowner who has not overlaid a bagua on the front garden, and they work regardless of which school the homeowner eventually uses.
How often do I redo the front garden review?
Twice a year is enough for most homeowners. The July 1 review sets the Ming Tang up for the Earth and Metal months. The January 1 review sets it up for the Water and Wood months. The two windows line up with the seasonal polarity shifts and give the homeowner two chances a year to make the big moves. Smaller adjustments — a new pot, a pruned hedge, a moved doormat — can happen in between, but the structural review is twice a year.
What if my partner and I disagree about the front garden?
The Ming Tang is a shared space, and the cycles are more forgiving than most homeowner-level articles admit. A front garden that one person has strong opinions about and the other person has no opinion about is a front garden in good feng shui order, because the qi is moving. A front garden that both people are fighting over is the one to fix first, before either person touches a plant. The five moves above are written for the homeowner, but the homeowner who shares the space should pick the one move they agree on and start there. The other four can wait.
