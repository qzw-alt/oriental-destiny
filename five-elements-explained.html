The Five Elements Explained

Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, Water. They are not ingredients and they are not symbols — they are five ways energy behaves. Here is what each one means and how they move.

Updated 2026-11-08 · about 10 minutes

Most Western explanations make the five elements sound like a mystical periodic table. They are simpler than that, and more useful. Each element is a pattern of behaviour — a way that energy tends to move.

Wood pushes upward and outward. Fire expands and rises. Earth settles and holds. Metal contracts and sharpens. Water flows downward and fills whatever it finds.

Once you see them that way, the rest of Chinese astrology stops being a list of symbols and starts being a description of how things move.

How they feed each other

The elements form a loop. Each one produces the next:

Wood feeds Fire — wood burns.
Fire makes Earth — ash becomes soil.
Earth makes Metal — ore is found in the ground.
Metal makes Water — condensation forms on cold metal.
Water feeds Wood — rain grows plants.

Then it starts again. This producing loop is the reason the system describes people as "supported" or "drained" — one element is always feeding another, and the balance of who feeds whom is a big part of what a chart reading is about.

How they control each other

There is a second loop, the one that keeps anything from growing without limit:

Wood breaks Earth — roots split soil.
Earth dams Water — a bank holds a river.
Water puts out Fire — obvious enough.
Fire melts Metal — heat changes metal.
Metal cuts Wood — the axe.

Producing and controlling together are what keep a chart from running away with itself. Too much of a good thing is a real problem in this system, and the controlling loop is the reason why.

An element is not good or bad. It is a behaviour. What matters is how much of it there is, and whether anything is keeping it in proportion.

Each element has two versions

Every element comes in a strong outward form and a softer inward one — that is what makes ten types rather than five. You will see both described below. They are the same element at different intensities, not two different things.

Wood — growth and direction

The tree (strong, outward) · the vine (soft, inward)

Wood is the energy of pushing upward. It plans, grows, and insists on a direction. Wood people tend to be idealistic, principled, and uncomfortable with being told to stop.

The tree version stands on its own and bends slowly — reliable, structural, sometimes rigid. The vine version climbs and adapts, getting where it needs to go by being flexible rather than forceful.

Wood is fed by Water and controlled by Metal. When Wood is strong in a chart, the person tends to be ambitious and forward-looking; when it is weak, they can struggle to start things or to hold a direction.

Common careers: planning, founding, teaching, architecture, law.

Fire — expression and visibility

The sun (strong, outward) · the candle (soft, inward)

Fire is the energy of expansion and display. It wants to be seen, to communicate, to warm the room. Fire people are expressive, and they usually know how to hold attention.

The sun version shines indiscriminately and can be exhausting to be around for long. The candle version is smaller and more focused, lighting one corner well — often more perceptive, and more easily drained.

Fire is fed by Wood and controlled by Water. Strong Fire reads as charisma and drive; weak Fire can read as difficulty expressing or being seen.

Common careers: performance, sales, leadership, teaching, anything public.

Earth — stability and nurture

The mountain (strong, outward) · the soil (soft, inward)

Earth is the energy that settles and holds. It is the most grounded of the five, and the most patient. Earth people are dependable, and they tend to be the ones others come to when things fall apart.

The mountain version is immovable and steady — sometimes too immovable. The soil version is fertile and nurturing, making things grow, and can give more than it should.

Earth is fed by Fire and controlled by Wood. Strong Earth reads as reliability; too much reads as inertia. Weak Earth can read as a struggle to feel settled.

Common careers: property, operations, care, hospitality, long-horizon work.

Metal — structure and precision

The blade (strong, outward) · the jewel (soft, inward)

Metal is the energy that contracts and sharpens. It is about standards, boundaries, and knowing exactly where the line is. Metal people are decisive and often have unusually clear judgment.

The blade version cuts quickly and can be blunt. The jewel version is refined and exacting, with beautiful taste and a painful eye for flaws — including its own.

Metal is fed by Earth and controlled by Fire. Strong Metal reads as decisiveness and integrity; too much reads as harshness or perfectionism.

Common careers: surgery, law, engineering, design, finance.

Water — depth and flow

The ocean (strong, outward) · the rain (soft, inward)

Water is the energy that flows downward and fills whatever shape it is given. It is the element of intelligence, adaptability, and going around obstacles instead of through them.

The ocean version is vast and powerful, comfortable with uncertainty and change. The rain version is subtle and penetrating, perceptive in ways that are hard to explain.

Water is fed by Metal and controlled by Earth. Strong Water reads as intelligence and range; too much reads as drifting. Weak Water can read as feeling stuck or unsure.

Common careers: research, writing, trading, counselling, anything fluid and fast.

What "balance" actually means

A common mistake is thinking the goal is to have all five elements equally. It is not. Most charts have one or two elements dominant, and that is normal — it is what makes a person distinct.

What matters is whether the dominant element is supported and kept in proportion. A chart with a lot of Fire is not "unbalanced" in a bad way if there is enough Water around to keep it from burning out, or enough Earth for it to rest in.

A useful way to hold it. Think of a good meal rather than a balanced equation. Nobody wants equal parts salt, acid, fat, and heat. They want the right amount of each for the dish. Charts work the same way.

How this applies to you

Your core element — the one that represents you — is called your Day Master. Everything else in your chart is described in relation to it. That is why the same element can mean different things for different people: a little Water is a relief to a strong Fire chart and a burden to a weak Earth chart.

Questions

Are the five elements literal?
No. They are not actual wood and fire — they are five descriptions of how energy behaves. Wood means growth and direction, not trees. The names are metaphors that have been consistent for a very long time.
Which element is the best one to be?
There isn't one. Every element has a strength and a matching weakness. Strong Fire is charismatic and can burn out. Strong Earth is reliable and can be stuck. The question is never which element is best, it is what your chart needs more or less of.
Do I have all five elements in my chart?
Usually, yes — the four pillars contain eight characters, each belonging to an element, so most charts contain several. Some charts are missing one entirely. A missing element is not a disaster; it just means that energy is less available to you by default.
What is the difference between the five elements and the ten gods?
The five elements describe the basic energy types. The ten gods describe how each of those energies relates to your core self. Same raw material, different layer of meaning. This page covers the raw material.
Can I change my elemental balance?
The birth chart is fixed. What changes is the environment — the directions you sleep and work in, the colours and materials around you, and the ten-year luck cycles that bring different elements forward. That is where feng shui and timing come in.
