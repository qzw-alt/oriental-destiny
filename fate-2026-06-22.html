Sitting and Facing in Feng Shui: How Compass School Reads Your Home's Direction

Yesterday's article covered the 24 Mountains, the ring on the luo pan that splits the compass into 15-degree sectors. Today's piece is what that ring is for. Every home in compass-school feng shui has two mountains — a facing mountain where the front door points, and a sitting mountain directly behind. The pair is the basis of almost every prescription the compass school will give you in 2026.

Published June 22, 2026 · 10 min read

What "sitting and facing" means, in plain language

In plain English, the facing of a home is the compass direction the front door points to. The sitting is the opposite direction, the mountain directly behind the home. If your front door opens to the south, your home faces south (the Wu mountain, 180 degrees on the 24 Mountains ring) and sits to the north (the Zi mountain, 0 degrees). The two are always 180 degrees apart, by definition.

That is the entire definition, and most of the time it is all you need. But the compass school treats the pair as a single unit, not as two separate directions. A south-facing, north-sitting home is not the same animal as a west-facing, east-sitting home. The pair carries a quality — the way the home "holds" qi, the way it receives energy from the front and the way it sends it out the back, the relationship between the receiving mountain and the supporting mountain. That quality is what the 24 Mountains ring is built to read.

The reason the pair matters is that most compass-school prescriptions are direction-specific. The 2026 Tai Sui sits in the southeast (Chen mountain). The 2026 Flying Star 5 Yellow sits in the center, with secondary positions in the east and the west. The 2026 wealth corner is in the south (Wu). None of these prescriptions is meaningful unless you can place them against the home's actual orientation. The sitting-and-facing pair is the orientation. Once you have it, the year's prescriptions land on a real home rather than on a generic compass.

Sitting and facing is just a way of naming the pair of opposite mountains a home sits between. The facing is the side the door points to; the sitting is the mountain behind. The pair is the canvas the rest of the compass-school system paints on.

How the pair is different from the form school

The form school (Ti Li, 形勢) reads the home by its physical environment — the mountains behind, the water in front, the road to the left, the open ground to the right. For a form-school practitioner, "facing" is not a compass reading at all. It is the direction the home "looks out" toward, which can be the front door, the largest window, the best view, or the open ground. The practitioner walks the land, and the land tells them where the home is facing.

The compass school (Liiqi Pai, 理氣) does the opposite. The direction is set by measurement, not by terrain. The front door is the facing, full stop, regardless of whether the door opens onto a garden, a parking lot, or a neighbor's wall. The mountain behind is the back wall, the back of the lot, the back of the building. The pair is geometric, not environmental.

For most Western readers, the form school sounds closer to what they have read about feng shui ("the commanding position," "the mountain behind, the water in front"). The compass school is the part that feels more technical — luo pans, mountain names, stem-branch cycles. The two are complementary, and a complete reading uses both. But the compass school is the part that drives the year-specific prescriptions (Tai Sui, Flying Stars, annual 5 Yellow), which is why the sitting-and-facing pair is the first thing a practitioner confirms when they walk into your home.

The four things practitioners check first

When a practitioner walks into a home for a reading, the first four questions are almost always: (1) where is the front door, (2) which direction does it face, (3) what is the mountain behind the home, and (4) is the home's measurement consistent with the street address's nominal orientation. The rest of the reading (the room-by-room prescriptions, the chart overlap, the year-specific stars) is layered on top of those four answers. If any of the four is wrong, the rest of the reading is off by a mountain, which is the resolution at which the prescription changes.

This is why practitioners carry a luo pan, not just a phone compass. The phone compass is accurate to within 2 to 3 degrees, which is fine for a quick sanity check but not fine for a sitting-and-facing reading, where a 5-degree error can land you in a different mountain. The luo pan reads to within a degree, and the practitioner uses that resolution to confirm the home's orientation against the street address, the lot orientation, and the practitioner's own eye.

The eight classical facing directions

The 24 Mountains ring gives you 24 possible facings, but the classical tradition groups them into eight canonical directions: the four cardinal (south, north, east, west) and the four intercardinal (southeast, northeast, southwest, northwest). Each canonical direction has a character that the practitioner reads at a glance.

The table reads top-to-bottom as the four cardinal pairs and bottom-to-top as the four intercardinal pairs. Most homes fall into one of the eight, because a home's facing is set by the street or the lot, and the street or the lot almost never points to a stem mountain (those sit in the 15-degree slices between the branches). A home whose door falls into a stem mountain is rare, and the practitioner treats it as a special case.

You can also read the table as four contrasts: south-facing/north-sitting homes (bright, expansive, with the year's Tai Sui diagonally behind) versus north-facing/south-sitting homes (quiet, inward, with the year's wealth corner diagonally behind); east-facing/west-sitting homes (growing, forward, with the year's secondary positions at the sides) versus west-facing/east-sitting homes (settling, contracting, with the year's 5 Yellow at the center). The classical tradition gives a character to each of the four contrasts, and the year's prescriptions are read against that character.

How to find your own sitting and facing

The actual reading takes about five minutes if you have a compass. Walk to your front door and stand just outside it, facing the direction the door opens to. Hold the compass level, let the needle settle, and read the degree. The degree is your facing. The opposite degree (facing plus 180, mod 360) is your sitting. Translate the degree to a mountain using yesterday's 24 Mountains table, and you have your pair.

For a quick translation without the full 24 Mountains table: any reading between 172.5 and 187.5 is the Wu mountain (south, 180 degrees). Any reading between 82.5 and 97.5 is the Mao mountain (east, 90 degrees). Any reading between 262.5 and 277.5 is the You mountain (west, 270 degrees). Any reading between 352.5 and 7.5 is the Zi mountain (north, 0 degrees). The intercardinal mountains fill the diagonal ranges. If your reading lands close to a boundary (172.5 is the boundary between Yi and Wu), the practitioner makes the call based on the home's lot and the practitioner's own eye.

There are three common errors that beginners make. The first is reading from inside the home, where the walls and the roof can deflect the compass by a degree or two. Walk outside, or stand in an open doorway. The second is reading from the side of the home, not the front. The facing is the side the door points to, not the side the door is mounted on. A door mounted on the south wall of a home that opens outward to the east is an east-facing door, because the door points east when it opens. The third is forgetting magnetic declination. Most phone compasses show true north by default, but older luo pans and some apps show magnetic north, and the difference can be 5 to 15 degrees depending on where you live.

What to do if your home faces a stem mountain

If your reading lands in a stem mountain (a 15-degree slice between two branches), the practitioner treats the home as facing the "leading edge" branch, the one on the side closer to the home's actual degree. A home at 165 degrees is technically in the Yi mountain (yin Wood, 157.5 to 172.5), but the practitioner reads it as a south-leaning home because 165 is closer to 180 (Wu) than to 157.5 (Mao). The reasoning is that the stem mountains are "in-between" energies, and the branch reading is the one that drives the prescriptions. A pure stem-mountain home (right on 165, or right on 195) is treated as a balanced edge case, with the practitioner noting both readings in the report.

How the 2026 prescriptions land on a south-facing, north-sitting home

A south-facing, north-sitting home (Wu facing, Zi sitting) is the most common orientation in the northern hemisphere, and the 2026 prescriptions read against it cleanly. The 2026 Tai Sui sits in the Chen mountain (southeast, 142.5 to 157.5), which falls to the front-left of a south-facing home. The prescription for the year is to keep the southeast sector quiet — no major renovations, no heavy noise, no long-term clutter accumulation. For a south-facing home, the southeast is a single diagonal step from the front door, which is the sector a practitioner walks into first.

The 2026 wealth corner (the Flying Star 8's annual position) sits in the Wu mountain (south), which is the home's facing mountain. The prescription is to keep the south sector active and clean — a clear sightline from the door, a strong light source, a piece of art that draws the eye. For a south-facing home, the wealth corner is the first thing a visitor sees when they walk in. The practitioner will often place a small water feature (a fountain, a fishbowl, a wall-mounted waterfall) in the south sector to support the year's energy.

The 2026 5 Yellow (the year's most volatile star) sits in the center, with secondary positions in the east (Mao) and the west (You). For a south-facing home, the east and west are the side walls — the practitioner prescribes quiet in both, with no construction, no heavy noise, and no accumulation of stagnant objects. The center of the home is the area around which the rooms arrange themselves, and the prescription is to keep the center open and clean.

A south-facing, north-sitting home is the most common orientation. In 2026, the south sector is the wealth corner, the southeast is the Tai Sui zone, and the east and west are the year's quiet sectors. The pair is the canvas; the year's prescriptions are the paint.

What changes if the home is east-facing, west-sitting

An east-facing, west-sitting home (Mao facing, You sitting) reads very differently. The 2026 wealth corner (Wu, south) is now to the right of the front door, not in front of it. The 2026 Tai Sui (Chen, southeast) is to the right-front. The east-facing home catches the morning sun, which is the day's most Yang moment, and the practitioner's reading of the year's energy reflects that. The home is naturally more "forward" in its energy — the year tends to push things outward, and the practitioner reads the year as one of expansion rather than consolidation.

For a west-facing, east-sitting home (You facing, Mao sitting), the picture inverts. The 2026 wealth corner is now behind the home (the south is the back-left of a west-facing home), and the 2026 Tai Sui is at the back-right (southeast is the back-left of a west-facing home). The west-facing home is the year's quiet side — the practitioner reads the year as one of inward consolidation, with the wealth corner behind the home rather than in front of it. The prescriptions are the same in spirit (keep the wealth corner active, keep the Tai Sui sector quiet), but the placement is different, and the placement is what the practitioner walks through with the homeowner room by room.

The intercardinal facings (southeast, northeast, southwest, northwest) read as combinations of the four cardinal characters. A southeast-facing, northwest-sitting home has the Yang Fire character of the south paired with the Yang Metal character of the northwest. The home is warm at the front, settled at the back, and the practitioner's reading of the year is a blend of the two. Most practitioners read intercardinal facings as 70-30 blends, weighted toward the cardinal direction the home's actual degree is closer to.

Frequently Asked Questions

What if my front door points to a different direction than the rest of the home?
For the purposes of the sitting-and-facing reading, the front door's direction is the facing, regardless of how the rest of the home is oriented. A home whose body sits east-west but whose front door opens to the south is read as a south-facing home. The reasoning is that qi enters through the door, and the door's direction is the direction the home is "facing" the world. If the home has a side entrance that gets more use than the front door, the practitioner reads the side entrance as the facing, with a note in the report. The two-door case is rare but it does come up, especially in older urban homes.
Does it matter if my home is on a hill?
It matters for the form school (the home's commanding position depends on whether it sits above or below the road), but not for the compass school. The compass school reads direction, not elevation. A south-facing home on a north-facing slope is still a south-facing home. The form school would add a note about the slope's influence on the home's qi (the slope either supports or drains the home's energy), but the compass-school reading is the same as for a home on flat ground.
What if I live in an apartment? My front door faces the building's corridor, not a real direction.
The compass school still reads the apartment by the front door's direction, not by the building's nominal orientation. A corridor-facing door that opens to the east is an east-facing apartment, regardless of which side of the building the apartment sits on. The reasoning is that the apartment is the home for the purposes of the reading, and the apartment's facing is the direction its door points. The form school would add notes about the apartment's position within the building (which floor, which side of the corridor, whether the building's sha qi from the elevator affects the home), but the compass-school reading is set by the door.
Can the sitting and facing change over time?
Yes, but only when the home's geometry changes. Adding a wing that becomes the new "front" of the home shifts the facing. Removing a wall to open the back of the home to a new view shifts the sitting. Most homes do not change geometry over a human lifetime, so the reading is stable. If the home is renovated significantly, the practitioner re-reads the sitting and facing. The annual prescriptions (Tai Sui, Flying Stars, 5 Yellow) are layered on top of a stable sitting-and-facing pair, and the pair itself is set by the home's geometry, not by the year.
What about a round home, or a home with no clear "front"?
Round homes, dome homes, and yurt-style homes are read by their entry. The entry is the facing; the point directly opposite is the sitting. The 24 Mountains ring is still 24 sectors, and the practitioner's reading is the same as for a square home whose door is at the same compass degree. The room-by-room prescriptions are more complex, because there is no clear "back wall" or "side wall" to layer the bagua onto, and most practitioners use the form school for the room-level work in a round home, with the compass school driving only the annual prescriptions.
Is there a quick way to remember the pair?
The mnemonic that practitioners use is "face the qi, sit on the mountain." The facing is the direction the home is "looking," and the qi is what the home is looking at. The sitting is the direction the home is "resting on," and the mountain is what the home is resting against. The pair is always 180 degrees apart, and the practitioner walks the home from the sitting (back) toward the facing (front) when they do the room-by-room reading. The order matters: you read from the back to the front, and the back is the foundation.
