Door Gods Feng Shui: Never Let Them Face Each Other (and 5 More Rules)

You have seen this pair. Two painted generals in armor, one with a sword and one with a mace, pasted on the two panels of the red door of every temple, every old courtyard house, every Chinese restaurant you have ever eaten in. They stand on the door all year and nobody explains who they are, why there are always two, or which way they are supposed to look. The direction is the whole point of the thing. 门神 (men shen), the door gods, are the one feng shui object with a face that watches — and they face outward, toward the street, away from you. This article covers who the pair is, why there must be two, why they face out instead of in, the 2026 threshold they are standing on, and the six rules that keep a guard from becoming a decoration.

This is the sixth stop in the object walk. The thread has read the Lucky Cat, who beckons fortune in, the Gold Ingot, who delivers it, the Wealth Bowl, who stores it, and the Lucky Knot, who binds it. Today the walk reads the pair who stand the other way — the money family's shield, the guardians who never sleep.

What the door gods are: the two who never slept

Start with the pair, because the pair is the first rule and the whole philosophy. There is no single door god. There are always two, one on each panel, and they are the two who stood watch so someone else could sleep. The oldest pair is 神荼 (Shen Tu) and 郁垒 (Yu Lei), the two brothers from the earliest ghost lore of the 山海经 (Shan Hai Jing), the Classic of Mountains and Seas. The story places them on the giant peach tree of Mount Dushuo, at the gate where the world of the living meets the world of ghosts. The brothers' one job was to inspect every spirit that tried to pass through, and any ghost that had done harm to the living they would bind and feed to the tiger that lived under the tree. The earliest door charm in the tradition was not a picture at all — it was the two brothers' names written on a piece of peach wood and hung at the gate. 桃符 (tao fu), the peach-wood talisman, is the ancestor of every door god who came after.

The pair most people paste today came later, out of a specific night in the Tang dynasty. The story is attached to 李世民 (Li Shimin), the Emperor Taizong, who after the violence that put him on the throne was haunted in his sleep by the ghosts of those he had wronged — the same blood that built the dynasty was now knocking at the bedroom door. Two of his generals, 秦琼 (Qin Qiong) and 尉迟恭 (Yuchi Gong), volunteered to stand guard outside the emperor's door, in full armor, through the night. The emperor slept. And so the generals stood the next night, and the next, until someone had the idea that would save their legs: paint the two generals' likenesses on the palace doors, and let the paintings stand the watch. It worked, the story says, and the practice left the palace for every household in the empire. Qin Qiong with his 双锏 (the twin maces) and Yuchi Gong with his 鞭 (the iron whip) are the pair you are seeing on the red doors. They are the reason the door gods are called the guardians who never sleep — the generals stayed awake so the house could rest.

The one-sentence mechanic: every object this month faces inward to bring fortune in. The door gods face outward to keep the blow out. Same door, two jobs, and the direction tells you which object you are looking at.

Read the pair before you paste it: martial and civil

The door gods are not one object, and "a pair on the door" is not a single instruction. The tradition splits the guardians by what they carry and which door they guard. The split matters because a martial pair on the bedroom door is a mistake, the same way the kylin is a mistake on the nightstand. Read the pair first, then place it.

The rule that follows: the outer door wants the martial pair, the inner doors want the civil pair, and the two are not interchangeable. The martial pair carries weapons and faces the street because the street is where the sha comes from. The civil pair carries a scroll and faces the study because the study is where the 文运 is earned. A martial general pasted on the inside of the bedroom door is the household putting a soldier on the night watch where a scholar belongs — the same yang-object rule the thread has repeated all month.

What the door gods do: they keep the door shut to the sha

Place the door gods in the object family and the direction they face becomes the entire explanation. The Lucky Cat beckons — paw raised, facing the door, calling wealth in. The Gold Ingot delivers — tips pointed in, sailing its cargo home. The Wealth Bowl stores — a harbor with the mouth to the room. The Lucky Knot binds — a rope tied around what has arrived. All four face inward, because their job is arrival. The door gods are the family's fifth, and only, outward-facing member: their job is not to bring the good in but to keep the bad out. They are the shield, the watch, the line that faces the street.

This is why the door gods belong in the same conversation as the thread's other guardians, and why they are the last watch on the same front line. The kylin faces the door from inside the house, the interior sentinel. The Taishan Stone anchors the threshold from behind, the stone that holds the gate shut. The bagua mirror hangs above the door and deflects whatever is aimed at it. The door gods are the threshold itself — the pair standing on the door, facing the street, the first thing the sha meets before it meets any of the others. They do not replace the kylin or the stone; they stand in front of them. Same front line, four posts, and the door gods are the outermost.

The facing rule is the one the article's title is built on, and it is the mistake I see most often. The two figures must face outward — toward the street, toward whatever is coming — and never each other, and never inward. A pair facing each other is two guards staring at their own hands while the door is unguarded. A pair facing inward is worse: it is the household pasting the greeter where the guard should be, and the greeter's job is to call things in, not keep them out. The Lucky Cat faces inward because it beckons. The door gods face outward because they do not beckon. The direction is the sentence.

The 2026 anchor: the threshold the trouble stars walk through

Every object in this August thread has needed its calendar hook, and the door gods get the most literal one of all: they are the one object that lives ON the door, and the door is where 2026's trouble enters. The site's own 2026 Flying Star read names three trouble stars this year — the 5 Yellow in the southwest (the worst, the star of accidents and losses), the 2 Black in the north (the star of illness), and the 3 Jade in the south (the star of quarrels). The sha those stars carry does not walk through walls. It walks through openings — doors and windows — and the front door is the largest opening in the house. The door gods face outward for exactly this reason: they meet the sha at the threshold, before it is inside.

The practical 2026 line follows from that. If your front door sits in, or faces, one of the three trouble sectors — the southwest, the north, or the south — the door gods are doing double duty this year: they are the traditional guardian AND the sector cure, the outward face on the exact threshold the year has marked. If your front door faces a straight road, a T-junction, or anything the tradition reads as a 路冲 (lu chong), a "road charge" aimed at the door, the door gods are the classical answer — the painted watch standing between the charge and the house. And if your front door is ordinary and unremarkable, the door gods still earn their place, because the door is the one threshold every household crosses every day, and it is the one place in the home where a guard is never redundant.

The door gods are a yang object — martial, outward, active — and the Metal month is the year's preview of the Metal that rules late 2026, the element of armor and of the weapons the generals carry. The practical line: inspect the door, settle the facing, and renew the pair before White Dew, while the month's Metal is still agreeing with the metal the guardians hold.

The placements below run from the essential to the optional. Only one of them is mandatory — the outer door — and the rest are refinements. None of them require buying anything you do not already have a version of; the intent is the activation, and the pair can be a New Year woodcut, a print, or even two respectful images of the threshold's meaning, so long as the direction and the pair are right.

The height and condition matter as much as the placement. The pair goes at eye level, not at the floor, where a guard should stand — two figures looking down the street, not up at the skirting board. They must be clean and bright, not faded or torn. A faded door god is a tired guard, and a torn one is a guard who has already taken a hit. The maintenance is the mechanism: the pair is replaced every Lunar New Year, the old pair burned, and the new pair pasted with the same intent the first one had.

Who gets the door gods: the chart layer

The door gods are a protection object, so the chart question is the protection question. In the Ten Gods, protection is the 印 (yin) — the Resource star, the element that produces and shields the day master, the mother, the teacher, the wall that stands between the day master and the blow. The 印 is the chart's built-in guardian, and the door gods are that star in paint: the home's 印, the external form of the internal protector. Find your own 印 by asking what produces your day master — Water produces Wood, Wood produces Fire, Fire produces Earth, Earth produces Metal, Metal produces Water — and that producing element is your chart's guardian element.

  • Wood day master (甲/乙). The 印 is Water. A strong Water 印 is the deep, patient, well-rooted tree; a weak one is the seedling with no root water. The door gods are the home's Water 印 in paint.
  • Fire day master (丙/丁). The 印 is Wood. A strong Wood 印 is a flame with a steady fuel supply; a weak one is a fire burning its own wick. The door gods feed the flame the shield it lacks.
  • Earth day master (戊/己). The 印 is Fire. A strong Fire 印 is warm soil that ripens; a weak one is cold clay. The door gods are the warmth on the threshold.
  • Metal day master (庚/辛). The 印 is Earth. A strong Earth 印 is ore held in the mountain until it is refined; a weak one is exposed ore. The door gods are the mountain face on the door.
  • Water day master (壬/癸). The 印 is Metal. A strong Metal 印 is a spring fed from a metal source; a weak one is a stream that dries in summer. The door gods are the metal source at the gate.

The practical read: a chart with a strong 印 reads as guarded — the person is naturally looked after, and the door gods are reinforcement, not necessity. A chart with a weak or absent 印 reads as exposed, and that is the person who benefits most from the guardian objects — the door gods, the kylin, the Taishan Stone — the external 印 that supplies what the chart does not. If you do not know your chart, the free reading names your day master, and the 印 is the one element one step back from it on the producing wheel.

The rules: six don'ts and five dos

The taboo list is the payload of this article, the section people save and forward. The first rule is the one nobody tells you and the reason this article has the title it has.

Don't (6)

  • Never let them face each other. Two guards staring at their own hands while the door is unguarded. Both figures face outward, toward the street, away from the house.
  • Never paste them facing inward. Inward-facing door gods become greeters, not guards — they beckon the sha in instead of keeping it out. The inward face is the Lucky Cat's job, not the door gods'.
  • Never hang a single door god. There is no singular door god. The pair is the mechanic — one on each panel, left and right, or the watch is incomplete.
  • Never put the martial pair in the bedroom. The yang, weapon-carrying guardian never goes in the yin rest of the bedroom, the same rule as the kylin and every pointed object this month.
  • Never leave a faded, torn, or peeling pair on the door. A tired guard is a worn-out shield. Replace at Lunar New Year, and renew a damaged pair when you notice it.
  • Never throw the old pair in the trash. Like the red string, the door gods are participants, not packaging. Burn the old pair — the clean return, Fire to Fire — and paste the new one with intent.

Do (5)

  • Paste a pair, at eye level, facing outward. Two figures, two panels, looking down the street — the essential post, and the direction is the whole sentence.
  • Match the pair to the door. Martial generals on the outer door for protection, civil scholars on the study door for 文运, the Three Stars on the inner family door.
  • Keep them clean and bright. Dry cloth, monthly, the same maintenance as the Lucky Cat. A bright guard is a working guard.
  • Renew at Lunar New Year. The pair is a yearly practice, not a permanent fixture. Out with the old — burned — in with the new, pasted with the sentence spoken.
  • Pair them with the threshold's other guards if the door is hard. A 路冲, a T-junction, a door in a trouble sector — the door gods, the bagua mirror, and the Taishan Stone are the layered answer, not one charm doing three jobs.

The honest calibration: what the door gods do not do

Every article in this thread ends with the limits, and the door gods' limits are worth stating plainly because the object looks so final.

First, they do not attract wealth — they keep out trouble. A pair on the door of a house with no income does not create income. That is the cat, the ingot, and the bowl's job. The door gods are the shield, not the harvest.

Second, they do not block every misfortune. They are a threshold guard, not a full-body suit. The rooms inside still need their own 2026 care — the southwest still wants stillness, the north still wants an empty counter, the south still wants one red object out. The door gods guard the door; the sector cures guard the rooms.

Third, they do not work faded. The mechanism is the maintenance, not the image. A torn, faded, or peeling pair is a tired guard, and a tired guard is a decoration. The yearly renewal is the practice; the paper is just its physical form.

Fourth, they are not a substitute for the door itself. A broken, misaligned, or permanently blocked front door is a structural problem the gods cannot fix. The guard stands on the door; if the door does not open and shut cleanly, fix the door first, then paste the pair.

Fifth, absence is not a curse. A house without door gods is a house without door gods. The tradition does not punish the unguarded; it rewards the guarding. There is no unlucky corner that a missing pair is failing to cover.

Frequently asked questions

Who are the two door gods?
Two pairs, one mythic and one historical. The mythic pair are 神荼 and 郁垒, the brothers from the Classic of Mountains and Seas who bound harmful ghosts at the gate of the peach tree. The pair pasted on most doors today are 秦琼 (Qin Qiong) and 尉迟恭 (Yuchi Gong), the two Tang-dynasty generals who stood guard all night so the Emperor Taizong could sleep. Both pairs are martial guardians; the second is simply the more common. There is no singular door god — the pair is the mechanic.
Do the door gods face inward or outward?
Outward, toward the street, away from the house. This is the single rule that matters, and the one people get wrong. The door gods keep the blow out, so they face whatever is coming. An inward-facing pair is a greeter, not a guard — the inward face belongs to the Lucky Cat, who beckons. Two figures facing each other are two guards staring at their own hands. Both face out, never each other, never in.
Do I need door gods on every door?
No. The outer door is the essential post — the threshold that faces the street and the sha. Inner doors are optional, and when they get a pair it is the civil 文门神 on the study or office door for the 文运 blessing, or the Three Stars on an inner family door. The martial pair belongs on the outer door and nowhere else. One guarded outer door does more than a pair on every inner door.
When do I replace the door gods?
At Lunar New Year, the traditional time — the pair is a yearly practice, not a permanent fixture. The old pair is burned, not thrown in the trash (Fire to Fire, the clean return, the same rule as the red string), and the new pair is pasted with the intent spoken. If a pair is torn or damaged before the New Year, renew it when you notice it, not when the calendar says to.
Can I use door gods in an apartment?
Yes. The apartment's front door is the threshold, and the same rules apply — a pair, at eye level, facing outward, renewed yearly. The only difference is scale: the apartment's "street" is the corridor outside the door, and the pair guards that crossing the same way a house pair guards the street. The door gods do not need a courtyard to have a threshold.
Do door gods conflict with a bagua mirror or a kylin?
No — they are layered, not competing. The bagua mirror hangs above the door and deflects what is aimed at it. The kylin faces the door from inside. The Taishan Stone anchors the threshold from behind. The door gods stand on the door itself, the outermost post. Same front line, different posts. Do not pile all of them on one door pointlessly — pick the one that fits the door's actual problem, and let the others stand where they earn their place.
