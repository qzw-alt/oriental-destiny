The Feng Shui Compass: 3 Rings That Tell You Where Your Home's Energy Actually Goes

The luo pan is not a fancy magnetic compass. It is a layered dial that turns a direction into a piece of information about the home, the mountain behind it, and the water in front of it. Most households do not need one. The ones that do usually know it within a month of moving in. Here is the difference.

Published June 19, 2026 · 9 min read

What a luo pan is

A luo pan (羅盤, luo pan, sometimes romanized as lo pan or compasses) is the traditional Chinese feng shui compass. It looks like a round brass or wooden disc, somewhere between the size of a dinner plate and a small pizza, with a magnetic needle in the center and a stack of concentric rings printed around the needle. Some are small enough to fit in a hand. Some are two feet across and meant to sit on a desk. The size does not matter. The rings do.

The first thing to know about a luo pan is that it is not a navigation tool. A magnetic compass tells you which way is north so you can walk there. A luo pan tells you which way is north so you can read the mountain sitting behind the home, the water sitting in front of it, and which of the 24 mountains is currently active in the sector where you plan to put a desk or a bed. The compass gives you one answer. The luo pan gives you twenty-four, layered in rings, each one narrowing the picture down a little further.

The second thing to know is that a phone compass is not a luo pan. The phone reads the magnetic field of the earth and tells you which direction the building is facing. That reading is necessary, but it is the first ring of a luo pan, not the whole instrument. Most of what the luo pan does happens after the phone compass has done its job, and the phone stops there.

A phone compass tells you which way is north. A luo pan tells you what that direction means for the home sitting on it. The first is geography. The second is feng shui.

The three rings that matter for most homes

A full traditional luo pan can have 30, 40, or even more concentric rings. The full instrument is a layered encyclopedia of compass school feng shui, with formulas for mountain stars, water stars, the 24 mountains, the 28 lunar mansions, the He Tu and Fu Xi diagrams, the trigrams, and the heavenly stems and earthly branches. The complete version is genuinely beautiful and genuinely something that takes years to read fluently. Most practitioners use three rings for most readings. The rest are for the specific moments when a question is narrow enough to need them.

Ring 1
24 Mountains
Divides the full circle into 24 named mountains, 15 degrees each, so a "southwest" reading becomes a specific named mountain rather than a vague quadrant.
Ring 2
Heavenly Stems & Earthly Branches
Lays the 10 stems and 12 branches around the dial so the compass direction can be matched to a BaZi pillar or an annual energy.
Ring 3
Early Heaven / Later Heaven Bagua
Places the eight trigrams in their two classical arrangements so the compass reading can be matched to either the cosmological (Early Heaven) or the applied (Later Heaven) layout.

These three rings are the working surface for most household feng shui. They are the rings you need to place a bed correctly, to align a desk with the year's helpful star, or to figure out whether the mountain behind the home is sitting in a sector that wants to be quiet this year. The other rings are useful. They are also the reason most people stop reading after the third.

The 24 mountains, and why 15 degrees is not the same as "south"

The 24 Mountains ring is the single most important layer of the luo pan, and it is the one most beginners find surprising. Instead of dividing the compass into four cardinal directions and four ordinal directions — the eight directions most people learn as children — the luo pan divides it into 24 named mountains, each 15 degrees wide.

The reason is that feng shui works in 15-degree increments, not 45-degree increments. A home with its main door facing "south" might in fact have the door facing due south, or 15 degrees east of south, or 30 degrees east of south, and each one of those three readings is a different mountain, with different energy, and a different prescription. A 45-degree quadrant cannot tell the difference. A 15-degree mountain can.

The 24 mountains are named for the 12 earthly branches (Zi, Chou, Yin, Mao, Chen, Si, Wu, Wei, Shen, You, Xu, Hai) plus the 8 trigrams (Qian, Kun, Zhen, Xun, Kan, Li, Gen, Dui) plus 4 special mountains (the four "corner" mountains at the cardinal transitions). Eight of the 24 mountains are considered "facing" mountains (the direction the front door looks toward) and sixteen are considered "sitting" mountains (the direction behind the home, including the mountain or hillside, the street, or the back wall of the building). The facing mountains are where the qi enters. The sitting mountains are where the qi rests.

For a household in 2026, the practical takeaway is that the 24 Mountains ring tells you whether the mountain behind your home is in a sector that wants stillness this year. If the back of your home sits in the southwest (the 5 Yellow sector of 2026), the mountain behind it is amplifying the 5 Yellow, and the back of the home wants the same stillness prescription as the southwest of the home. A phone compass cannot tell you that. The 24 Mountains ring can.

A 45-degree quadrant gives you "southwest." A 15-degree mountain gives you "Gen, the southwest transition, sitting on Kun's shoulder." The second is what the year is asking about.

How to take a reading

Taking a luo pan reading is not a casual gesture. The instrument is precise, and small mistakes in the reading produce readings that are confidently wrong. The procedure below is the one I use, taught to me by a teacher who learned it in Hong Kong in the 1970s, and it is roughly the procedure that most Compass school practitioners follow.

  1. Stand in the center of the home, or as close to the center as you can get. The reading is for the whole home, not for the bedroom or the front door. If the home has multiple floors, take the reading on the floor where the family spends the most waking hours. For most households, that is the ground floor.
  2. Hold the luo pan level, with both hands on the rim. The instrument has to be flat for the magnetic needle to settle correctly. A tilted reading adds several degrees of error, and several degrees is the difference between one mountain and the next.
  3. Wait for the needle to stop moving. This takes between 30 seconds and 2 minutes, depending on the luo pan and the magnetic environment. Do not read the needle while it is still swinging. The reading is the settled position, not the average position.
  4. Read the 24 Mountains ring first. The mountain the needle points to is the mountain the home is sitting on. The opposite mountain is the mountain the home is facing. Write both down. You will need the facing mountain to read the year ahead.
  5. Read the Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches ring second. Note which stem and which branch sit at the needle's position. This is the BaZi correspondence for the home, and it can be compared to your personal BaZi chart to find the directions that agree with the chart and the directions that argue with it.
  6. Read the bagua ring last. Note which trigram the needle points to. The trigram gives you the sector's elemental nature (Kan = Water, Li = Fire, Zhen = Wood, Xun = Wood, Gen = Earth, Kun = Earth, Qian = Metal, Dui = Metal) and confirms whether the 24 Mountains reading and the bagua reading agree.

Once you have the three numbers, you can do most of the household feng shui that the year requires. The Flying Star placement of 2026 (covered in last week's article) goes on the compass reading from the 24 Mountains ring. The bed direction for a person with a given Day Master comes from the Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches ring. The element balance for the home comes from the bagua ring. The three numbers together are the home's address in compass school feng shui.

When a household needs a luo pan

Most households do not need a luo pan. A phone compass is enough for most of what a household is going to face in a year, especially for the three trouble sectors of 2026 (the southwest, the north, and the south) and the two helpful sectors (the east and the northwest). The phone gives you the quadrant, the quadrant is enough for the prescriptions above, and the prescriptions work without the precision of the luo pan.

A luo pan becomes worth owning when one of three things is true. First, the home sits at an unusual angle to compass north — not facing due south or due east, but rotated 10, 15, or 25 degrees off. These homes are common in hillside neighborhoods, in subdivisions laid out after the original survey, and in apartments built into buildings that were themselves built at an angle to the grid. A phone compass reads the angle, but cannot tell you which mountain is active within the angle.

Second, the household is going through a year that demands precision — a year of major renovation, a year of buying or selling, a year of starting a new business, or a year of a difficult health decision. These years are the ones where the difference between one mountain and the next is the difference between a helpful placement and a neutral one. The luo pan is the instrument that gives you the helpful placement.

Third, the practitioner is a serious student of the Compass school and wants to read the annual Flying Stars with the precision the system was designed for. The Flying Stars rotate through 15-degree mountains. A 45-degree quadrant reading loses three mountains of precision on each side. The luo pan recovers the precision.

Buy a luo pan when the answer to "what direction is the mountain behind my home" changes the prescription. Most households, most years, that question has the same answer at 45 degrees as it does at 15.

What to look for in a first luo pan

If you decide to buy one, three things matter. First, the rings should be legible — not so small that you need a magnifying glass, not so large that the instrument does not fit on a flat surface. A diameter between 15 and 25 centimeters is the working range for most adults.

Second, the magnetic needle should settle within a minute and a half when held level. A sluggish needle usually means a low-quality magnet, and a sluggish needle produces readings that are off by 2 or 3 degrees. That is the difference between two adjacent mountains.

Third, the luo pan should include the 24 Mountains ring, the Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches ring, and at least one bagua ring. These are the three working rings for household feng shui. A luo pan without them is decorative, not functional. A luo pan with them, plus a He Tu ring or a Fu Xi ring, is a serious instrument that will outlive most of the other things in the household.

Two days before Xia Zhi: what to do with the reading before the solstice

This article is going up two days before Xia Zhi (夏至, Summer Solstice, June 21, 2026). The summer solstice is the moment the sun reaches its highest point in the northern sky, and in classical feng shui the solstice is one of the four annual checkpoints for the compass reading of a home. The other three are the winter solstice (Dong Zhi), the spring equinox (Chun Fen), and the autumn equinox (Qiu Fen). At each checkpoint, the year's Flying Stars settle into their working positions and the home's compass reading becomes most relevant for the next three months.

If you take a luo pan reading between Li Chun and Xia Zhi, you are reading the late-spring configuration of the home. If you take the reading between Xia Zhi and Li Qiu, you are reading the summer configuration, and the two readings can differ by a few degrees on the 24 Mountains ring because of the sun's apparent motion through the sky. The classical advice is to take the reading on the solstice itself, or as close to it as possible. The 21st is the working window. The 19th through the 23rd is close enough.

The practical move for the next few days, if you have a luo pan, is to take the reading on Saturday or Sunday morning, write down the three numbers from the three rings, and compare them to where the family is sleeping, working, and eating in 2026. If the mountain behind the home is in a sector that wants stillness this year (the southwest, in 2026), the back of the home wants the same stillness prescription as the southwest of the home. If the front of the home faces a sector that wants less red this year (the south, in 2026), the front door and the entryway want the same red-removal prescription as the south of the home. The luo pan gives you the precision. The summer solstice gives you the timing.

Common mistakes that turn a luo pan reading into wrong advice

There are a few mistakes that show up in roughly half of the home readings I see from people who have just bought their first luo pan. None of them are hard to fix. All of them produce readings that look right and prescribe things that do not work.

The first is reading the magnetic needle instead of the 24 Mountains ring. The magnetic needle points to magnetic north, not to true north. The difference is small in most of the eastern United States (a degree or two) but can be 10 degrees or more in the western United States and in much of Europe. The luo pan has both readings printed on it — a magnetic north marker and a true north marker. Use the true north marker. The magnetic needle is for orientation, not for placement.

The second is taking the reading from the front door. The front door is the most convenient place to stand, and the door is the place where the family enters and exits, but the door is not the center of the home. A reading taken at the door is a reading of the door, not of the home. The reading has to come from the center. If the home has no clean center, take the reading from the room where the family spends the most time.

The third is reading the luo pan while wearing a watch with a metal band, or while standing next to a refrigerator, a metal door frame, or a large speaker. Metal interferes with the magnetic needle, and the interference adds 3 to 5 degrees of error to the reading. Take off the watch. Move away from the fridge. Read the instrument in the middle of the room, with the nearest metal object at least a meter away.

The fourth is making a decision based on a single reading. Take the reading three times, on three different days, at roughly the same time of day. If the three readings agree to within 2 degrees, the answer is reliable. If the three readings disagree by 5 or more degrees, something is off — usually metal in the floor or a recent renovation that moved a large piece of metal — and the reading needs to be re-taken from a different spot in the home.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a luo pan the same as a regular magnetic compass?
No — a magnetic compass has one ring and one needle, and tells you which way is north. A luo pan has between 20 and 40 concentric rings, and tells you which mountain the home is sitting on, which BaZi pillar corresponds to the direction, and which trigram is active in the sector. The magnetic compass is a tool for navigation. The luo pan is a tool for placement. A phone compass is closer to a magnetic compass than to a luo pan. Most of what a luo pan does is the work that happens after the compass needle has settled.
My phone compass disagrees with my luo pan by a few degrees. Which one is right?
Both, mostly — the phone reads the magnetic field directly and gives you the magnetic north reading. The luo pan has both the magnetic north marker and the true north marker printed on the dial, and the difference between the two is the magnetic declination for your location. The declination is small (a degree or two) in most of the eastern United States, larger (10+ degrees) in the western United States, and the luo pan corrects for it automatically. Trust the luo pan for placement decisions. Trust the phone for quick orientation. The two are answering slightly different questions.
Do I need a luo pan if I already follow the bagua map on my wall?
No — the bagua map is a placement tool that divides the home into nine sectors and assigns each sector an area of life. The luo pan is a compass tool that tells you which sector is which direction. The two work together. The bagua map needs the luo pan (or the phone compass) to know which sector is which. The luo pan benefits from the bagua map to translate the compass reading into a placement decision. A household that uses only the bagua map is reading a placement diagram without knowing which way is north. A household that uses only the luo pan is reading a compass without knowing what to do with the reading. The two together are the working system.
How much does a good first luo pan cost?
A working luo pan with the three rings (24 Mountains, Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches, and one bagua ring) runs between $80 and $300 for most adults buying one for home use. The inexpensive end is usually a smaller diameter (10-15 cm) with legible rings and a functional magnetic needle. The expensive end is usually a larger diameter (20-30 cm) with more rings, a wooden case, and a heavier needle. Both are functional. The smaller one is easier to read quickly. The larger one is easier to read accurately. Most first-time owners start with the smaller one and graduate to the larger one if they end up using it weekly.
Is the luo pan only used in the Compass school of feng shui?
Yes — the Form school (Ti Li, 形勢) reads the land, the water, the road, and the mountain by sight, without an instrument. The Compass school (Liiqi Pai, 理氣) reads the direction by compass, with the luo pan. The two schools disagree about which one matters more, and serious practitioners learn both. The luo pan is the working instrument of the Compass school. The Form school uses the eye. A household that follows the Form school does not need a luo pan. A household that follows the Compass school does.
Can a luo pan help me place a desk or bed better than a phone compass?
Yes, if the difference between one mountain and the next changes the prescription. For most households, the prescription is the same at 15 degrees as it is at 45 degrees — the southwest wants stillness, the east wants activity, the south wants less red. Those three prescriptions work at the quadrant level. If the household is placing a desk for a person whose Day Master is sensitive to a specific mountain (a Geng Day Master, for example, benefits from a specific stem-and-branch direction), the 15-degree precision is what makes the prescription work. For the rest of the placements, the phone is enough.
