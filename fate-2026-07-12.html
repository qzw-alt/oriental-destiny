Apartment Feng Shui for July: Reading the Three Gates When You Don't Own the Lot

Yesterday's three-gate article walked the house through its outer gate, its inner gate, and its mouth gate. Today's piece is the same walk for the homeowner who owns the unit but not the lot. The outer gate becomes a building lobby the homeowner can't move. The property line becomes a wall shared with the neighbor. The mouth gate is the only gate the renter fully controls. A thirty-minute apartment pass that fixes what can be fixed and reads the rest clearly.

Published July 12, 2026 · 12 min read

Why the apartment read needs its own article

The three-gate article from yesterday opened with the settled/compressed/depleted vocabulary and walked the homeowner through the gate reads in a single twenty-minute walk. The walk was written for a house. The walk assumed the homeowner could move the front fence if the outer gate was depleted. The walk assumed the homeowner owned the driveway, the front yard, the curb cut, and the path to the sidewalk. None of those assumptions hold for an apartment. The apartment has its own versions of all three gates, and the apartment versions are stacked in a way that the house versions are not.

The stacked-gate problem is the article's reason for existing. In a house, the three gates are three separate thresholds — the homeowner can stand at each one and look at it deliberately. In an apartment, the outer gate (the building's front door) and the inner gate (the apartment's own door) are stacked within twenty feet of each other, and the two read together as one gate the homeowner crosses twice. The mouth gate, in a one-bedroom apartment, may be four feet from the inner gate. The apartment homeowner is reading three thresholds that all sit in the same room.

The article exists separately from yesterday's because the apartment read has its own vocabulary. The outer gate is a gate the renter reads but does not own — the building super or the management company owns the gate, and the renter's job is to read it accurately even when the renter cannot move it. The inner gate is the apartment door the renter owns — this is the gate the apartment article spends the most time on, because this is the gate most apartment feng shui guides get wrong. The mouth gate is the same gate as in a house, but the mouth gate in a studio apartment is the same physical threshold as the inner gate, and the collapsed mouth gate is the most common apartment feng shui failure mode.

The apartment read, in one sentence The outer gate is the building's front door, which the renter reads but doesn't move. The inner gate is the apartment door, which the renter reads and does move. The mouth gate is the opening between the apartment's entry and the room the renter lives in, which collapses into the inner gate in studios and stays separate in one-bedrooms.

The outer gate in an apartment: read what you can't move

The outer gate in an apartment is the threshold between the public way (the street, the sidewalk, the parking lot, the bike rack, the building's front path) and the building's lobby. For a walk-up, the outer gate is the building's front door at street level. For an elevator building, the outer gate is the lobby door (or the first set of automatic doors before the lobby). For a building with a concierge, the outer gate is the desk itself — the concierge's line of sight is the line the dragon has to cross. The outer gate is the only apartment gate the renter reads but cannot move, and it is the gate most apartment feng shui guides skip because there is nothing the renter can do about it. The read still matters, because the renter who knows the outer gate is depleted can compensate at the inner gate.

The outer gate read is the simplest apartment read. The renter walks to the building's front entrance from the public side (from across the street, from the parking lot, from the bus stop where the renter catches the elevator). What the renter sees is the outer gate read. A wide entrance with the lobby visible — automatic doors that part to show a clean lobby, a glass door that lets the renter see straight into the elevator bank — is the settled read. A narrow entrance with a turnstile the renter has to push through is the compressed read. An entrance the renter can't see the street from (a side door at the back of the building, a service door the renter uses because the front door is broken or restricted) is the depleted read.

The fix at the outer gate is not the renter's to make, and the apartment read says so out loud. A depleted outer gate — a building whose front entrance is at the back of the lot, a building whose lobby door faces a wall, a building the renter cannot enter without going around the block — is a gate the renter reads as depleted and compensates for at the inner gate. The compensation is the apartment article's most useful move. The renter who has a depleted outer gate treats their apartment door as the primary outer gate, and the building's lobby door as a secondary threshold the renter doesn't try to fix. The renter who has a settled outer gate treats the apartment door as the inner gate in yesterday's three-gate sense. The read at the building door tells the renter which interpretation to use, and that one decision changes every other read in the apartment.

The single most useful move at a depleted outer gate is the lobby-walk. If the building has a back lobby, a side entrance, a service door the renter is allowed to use, a courtyard the renter crosses — the renter walks that path on a Saturday morning and looks at what the dragon sees. The lobby-walk is the read. A building with a depleted street-facing outer gate and a settled courtyard walk-through still has a depleted outer gate from the dragon vein's perspective, but the renter using the courtyard regularly is reading the gate through the courtyard rather than the street. The renter's choice of entry path is the outer gate adjustment most apartment homeowners can make.

The inner gate in an apartment: the door that does the work

The inner gate in an apartment is the apartment's own front door — the door the renter has the keys to, the door the renter opens and closes every day, the door that locks behind the renter on the way out and locks behind the renter on the way in. The inner gate is the gate the apartment article spends the most time on, because it is the gate most apartment feng shui guides treat as identical to a house's front door. The apartment's inner gate is not identical. The apartment's inner gate sits inside another set of doors (the lobby door), and the apartment's inner gate is the renter's last line of separation between the public side and the private side. The read matters more in an apartment than in a house because the apartment's inner gate carries the weight of two gate reads in a row.

The inner gate read is the read the renter does while looking in the apartment door. The renter opens the apartment door from the outside (the hallway side), steps across the threshold, and turns to look back at the door from inside. What the renter sees is the inner gate read. A door that opens onto a foyer or a hallway with a sight line to the back window is the settled read. A door that opens onto a hallway that turns sharply within ten feet is the compressed read. A door that opens onto a wall (the door is three feet from the kitchen wall, the closet door is directly across from the apartment door, the bathroom door is the first thing the renter sees) is the depleted read.

The single move at the inner gate that fixes more apartment feng shui failures than any other: close every interior door before opening the apartment door. The renter's kitchen door, bathroom door, closet door, and bedroom door should all be closed when the renter comes home, opens the apartment door, and walks in. Closing the interior doors before the apartment door is opened keeps the dragon from seeing the bathroom or the storage closet as the first thing on the apartment side of the threshold. The closed-door version of the read is settled; the open-door version of the same read is depleted. The action takes five seconds and changes the gate read without moving any furniture.

The mirror read at the apartment's inner gate is the second-most-useful move. A mirror directly facing the apartment door reflects the dragon back out before it can enter the unit. A mirror on the side wall of the entry (parallel to the line from the apartment door into the apartment's deepest point) reflects the dragon deeper into the unit. A mirror on the wall behind the renter when the renter stands at the apartment door has no effect — it is decoration. The mirror placement that helps the inner gate is the mirror placement the apartment owner can choose freely, because the apartment owner controls their own walls. The mirror on the side wall is the apartment owner's friend at the inner gate.

The doormat is the third apartment-specific inner gate move. A doormat at the apartment door is the deliberate line the dragon crosses between the public hallway and the private unit. The doormat is the apartment's version of the front yard's curb cut — a marked threshold where the gate is. A doormat that is wider than the door (extending at least two feet to each side) is settled. A doormat that is narrower than the door is compressed. No doormat is depleted. The doormat is the cheapest apartment feng shui fix on the market, and the one most apartment feng shui guides skip because doormats look like decoration rather than gate-marking. The doormat is the gate, not the decor.

The apartment's inner gate is the gate the renter owns. It is also the gate the renter is least likely to read deliberately, because the renter crosses it twice a day without thinking. The thirty-second pause — close the interior doors, plant the side-wall mirror, lay the wide doormat — is the read. The apartment renter who has done those three moves has done more apartment feng shui than the renter who has bought the lucky bamboo and hung the calligraphy scroll.

The mouth gate in a studio: when it collapses

The mouth gate in a one-bedroom apartment is the opening between the entry hallway and the living room (or the opening between the entry and the kitchen in a one-bedroom with a galley). The mouth gate in a studio apartment is the opening between the apartment door and the main room — and in a studio, this opening is the same threshold as the inner gate. The collapsed gate is the studio apartment's defining feng shui problem, and the problem the apartment article has to address separately from the one-bedroom version.

The collapsed mouth gate reads as depleted by default. The renter who walks into a studio walks past the apartment door and into the same room the renter sleeps in, cooks in, works in, and entertains in. There is no separate mouth gate threshold; the apartment door opens onto the bed, the couch, the desk, the kitchen — all of them. The dragon crosses from the public hallway into the renter's entire life in a single step. The collapsed mouth gate is the failure mode that reads as "I live here but the apartment doesn't feel like mine" — the same phrase yesterday's article used for the blocked mouth gate in a house. The collapsed mouth gate in a studio is the same failure mode, with the fix being to deliberately define the threshold that doesn't physically exist.

The studio mouth gate fix is the deliberate-marking move from yesterday's FAQ. The renter puts down a rug. The rug is the threshold the studio doesn't have. A rug placed inside the apartment door that covers at least the first six feet of the studio floor is the mouth gate. A rug that sits in the middle of the studio (under the coffee table, in front of the couch, centered on the bed) is not the mouth gate — it is the room's center mark, not its threshold mark. A rug placed deliberately inside the front door, oriented so the renter's first step lands on the rug, is the studio's mouth gate, and the rug is doing the work a wall would do if the studio had walls.

The single move that completes the studio mouth gate is the screen, the bookshelf, the curtain — whatever piece of furniture the renter plants at the line the rug defines. A screen three feet inside the apartment door, perpendicular to the door, with the rug running from the apartment door to the screen's base — that screen is the studio's mouth gate. A bookshelf on the same line does the same work. A curtain hung from the ceiling on the same line does the same work without taking up floor space. The studio renter who plants a deliberate threshold marker inside the apartment door has fixed the collapsed mouth gate. The renter who hasn't is reading the studio as depleted at the mouth gate by default.

The day-master element at the studio mouth gate follows the same rule as the house mouth gate from yesterday: Wood-heavy charts get a tall plant; Fire-heavy charts get a warm-toned element; Earth-heavy charts get nothing (Earth is the studio's own element); Metal-heavy charts get a metal element; Water-heavy charts get a glass element. The studio mouth gate is the place where chart-specific element work pays the highest dividends, because the studio has no other place the element can live. The studio renter's chart and the studio's mouth gate are the same conversation.

The thirty-minute apartment pass

The apartment pass is one continuous walk that takes thirty minutes, a notepad, and the deliberate-read intention the three-gate article built. The walk has eight steps, and each step takes under four minutes. By the end of the walk the renter has a one-line read for each of the three gates, a one-line fix for any gate that read compressed, and a one-line calendar entry for any gate that read depleted. The walk is the read.

  1. Walk to the building's front entrance from across the street. Note what you see. Wide lobby visible through glass = settled. Turnstile = compressed. Side door or back path = depleted. One word.
  2. Walk to the building's front entrance using whatever path you use day-to-day. Note whether the day-to-day path crosses the public side, the courtyard, or both. The path the renter walks every day is the outer-gate read that matters.
  3. Stand in the hallway outside the apartment door. Look at the apartment door. Note whether the door faces the elevator bank directly (depleted), faces a side hallway (compressed), or faces an end of the hallway with a window (settled). One word.
  4. Close every interior door (kitchen, bathroom, closet, bedroom) before opening the apartment door. Stand inside the apartment door and look at the apartment. A foyer or hall with a sight line to the back window = settled. A hallway turning sharply = compressed. A wall directly across the doorway = depleted. One word.
  5. For the inner gate, check the side-wall mirror. A mirror directly facing the apartment door = depleted at the mirror read. A mirror on the side wall (parallel to the deepest line of sight) = settled. No mirror = the renter's choice to make.
  6. For the inner gate, check the doormat. A doormat wider than the door = settled. A doormat narrower than the door = compressed. No doormat = depleted at the mat-line. The doormat is the cheapest fix on the floor.
  7. For the mouth gate, check the rug. A rug inside the apartment door, sized for the first six feet of floor, oriented so first-step lands on it = settled. A rug in the middle of the room = no mouth gate. No rug = depleted at the threshold line.
  8. For the studio, check the screen or curtain. A deliberate threshold marker inside the apartment door, perpendicular to the door, marking the same line the rug defines = settled mouth gate. No marker = collapsed mouth gate. The marker is the wall the studio doesn't have.
The apartment pass, in one sentence Read the building's front entrance (the outer gate you don't own). Read the apartment door with all interior doors closed (the inner gate you do own). Mark the threshold inside the apartment door with a rug and a screen (the mouth gate that may need to be defined). The apartment renter who has done those three reads has done the July thread at home.

Frequently Asked Questions

My apartment is on a high floor with a great view. Does the view compensate for a depleted outer gate?
No, but in a specific way. The view from the apartment's windows is a separate read from the three-gate read — the view belongs to the apartment's interior, not to the gates. A depleted outer gate (a building lobby that faces a wall) stays depleted regardless of what the apartment's windows show. The apartment feng shui work the renter can do is at the inner gate (the apartment door) and the mouth gate (the threshold inside the door), and those reads happen entirely on the renter's side of the building's front door. The view is the apartment's blessing; the gate is the renter's work. A great view and a depleted inner gate can coexist; the renter's job is to make the inner gate settle with a wide doormat and a side-wall mirror, regardless of what the windows show.
What if I'm on the ground floor and my windows face the building's front path? Is my apartment's window a gate?
It depends on whether the renter uses the window as an entry. The dragon enters through doorways and gates the renter crosses daily; it does not enter through windows the renter only looks through. A ground-floor apartment with a window the renter never opens is just a window — the read is the daylight, not the gate. A ground-floor apartment with a window the renter uses as a back door (the renter steps through the window into a private garden, a patio, a fenced yard the renter owns the use of) is a fourth gate, and the four-gate read becomes the apartment's read. The window-as-gate is a read the renter sets up by using the window as a daily entry, not a read the renter can take credit for by simply having a ground floor.
Is there any apartment feng shui move I should NOT do?
Yes — three. Do not hang a mirror directly facing the apartment door (it sends the dragon back out). Do not place a large piece of furniture (a bookshelf, a tall cabinet) directly behind the apartment door the door swings into (it shortens the inner gate's swing path and damps the read). Do not hang a wind chime on the apartment door itself (the hallway side of an apartment door doesn't belong to the renter, and a wind chime in a shared hallway is also a neighbor-relations move that backfires more often than it helps). The three "do nots" are the apartment-specific version of the "do not stick the dragon vein where it can't run" rule from the four-line article. The apartment renter who has hung the mirror on the side wall, kept the door's swing clear, and kept the chime off the hallway door has done more apartment feng shui than the renter who has the lucky bamboo by the window and the feng shui coins over the apartment door.
How does the apartment read change if I share the apartment with a roommate?
The inner gate and the mouth gate stay the same — the apartment door and the threshold inside it are the renter's gates regardless of who else lives there. The roommate-specific feng shui question is whether the apartment door is read as a single gate (one person crosses it first, the second crosses after) or as a paired gate (two people cross together). Most roommates cross the apartment door sequentially, and the sequential read is the same as a single-renter read. Two roommates who cross together (one opens the door, the other follows within five seconds) read the gate as paired — the gate has a slightly heavier load, and the doormat should be wider for a paired read than for a single read. The roommate-augmented apartment feng shui rule is one wide doormat, one side-wall mirror, one rug at the inner line — the same three moves, executed for two readers.
My building has a concierge. Does the concierge count as the outer gate?
Yes, in the specific sense that the concierge's line of sight is the read. A concierge who greets every entrant by name, who knows which resident is coming home by the sound of the door, who tracks the lobby traffic — that concierge is a settled outer gate. A concierge who sits behind plexiglass, who barely looks up from a phone, who tracks only deliveries — that concierge is a depleted outer gate (the dragon can pass without being read). A concierge the resident has never spoken to is a blocked outer gate (the dragon enters a lobby the resident has no read on). The concierge read is independent of the building's physical entrance — a building with a narrow entrance but a present concierge is settled; a building with a wide entrance but an absent concierge is depleted. The concierge is the read the apartment renter usually skips because the concierge is treated as decoration rather than threshold. The concierge is the threshold.
I rent a room in a shared apartment (not a whole unit). Does the apartment read still apply?
Partially. The renter who rents a room in a shared apartment owns the room door, not the apartment door. The room door is a separate gate — the outer gate is the apartment's front door (which the room-renter reads but doesn't move), the inner gate is the room door (which the room-renter owns), and the mouth gate is whatever the renter has set up inside the room. The room read is a smaller-scale version of the apartment read: a rug inside the room door, a closed interior door before opening the room door, a mirror on the side wall of the room's entry line. The room-renter who treats the room as a separate apartment has the apartment feng shui pass at room scale, and the pass takes fifteen minutes rather than thirty. The principles hold. The scale shrinks.
